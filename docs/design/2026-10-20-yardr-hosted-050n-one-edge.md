# One edge for hosted yards and for yards that connect out to it

Design yardr-hosted-050n, proposed 2026-10-20. A proposal as it was decided on, not a description of what the code does today; the decision is on the bead.

A yard is one installation of yardr: its store, its crew and its depots. The operator is the person who runs it. A device is one program of a user that calls the yard from outside it, such as footplate, the native window over a yard. The hook listener is the yard's one network listener, set by `hook_addr`, serving `/hooks/`, `/peers/` and `/user/`. The edge is the service at https://my.yardr.dev that stands in front of yards: today Kratos (accounts), Ory's reference sign-up pages, a provisioner and our own router, in the repository `yardr-hosted` (design yardr-bwm2). A hosted yard is one we run for a person, in a container or a droplet of their own. A connected yard is new in this design: a yard the person runs on their own machine that keeps one outbound connection open to the edge, which we call the link. See docs/glossary.md for the rest of yardr's words.

## 1. The question

mckean, 20 Oct 07:20, after the strategy document "Yardr — Monetization & Licensing Strategy" (revised 18 Oct): free to run yourself, paid to stay connected; "reminds me of obsidian" (a free local app, paid Sync). What is new: "a self-hosted yard gets easy hosted webhooks and on-the-go connectivity without open ports." Today the yard takes webhooks on `0.0.0.0:8790`, which needs a reachable port or a tunnel; footplate pairs on the LAN, or through the edge only for a hosted yard; peers link over networks the user controls. The design is to say what carries the yard's outbound connection to the edge, how it authenticates with the yard key and how it reconnects; how the edge gives each yard a URL per hook and forwards the raw body and headers so the yard verifies signatures itself, with signing secrets never at the edge; how footplate and the web view reach the yard through the same connection; what the edge can read (webhook payloads and yard events, said plainly, not claimed end-to-end) and what it never holds (repositories, model credentials); how it fits the hosted sign-up and the Google mint of yardr-hosted-4dx0; and the smallest first slice. At 07:30 he added: "hosted yards are in the offering too", so one edge serves both kinds; the design says what the two share (sign-up, accounts, webhook URLs, footplate pairing) and where they differ, and for hosted yards carries the strategy's limit, API keys or provider plans whose terms allow hosted automated use, and how sign-up checks it. At 07:40 (relayed by the yardmaster): the connection could be a separate app, perhaps TypeScript; it must work with no port forwarding and on laptops that change networks. Left out by the bead: billing, teams and Junction. Kept: mckean's rule for networking (memory mem-stdz), "define what to allow, … we're not building nginx"; this is a service we run, not proxy configuration in the yard, and the design says how it keeps that line.

## 2. What exists today

### 2.1. The edge already routes webhooks per yard, by path

`yardr-hosted/router/internal/router/router.go`, `serveHooks`: a request to `/hooks/<handle>/<hook>` looks the handle up in the provisioner's map (`internal/edgemap/map.go`, a SQLite file read through `sqlite3`; rows carry `Backend` `docker` or `droplet`, `Target`, `State`), strips `/hooks/<handle>`, and proxies to that yard's hook listener at `http://<target>:8790` (`DefaultYardBase`). Webhook deliveries carry no session by design; the yard verifies them. Every other yard route (the web view, `/user/*`) needs a Kratos session: the browser's cookie, or footplate's session token in `X-Edge-Token` (design bwm2, 3.6), and the router mints a one-minute JWT for the web view (`internal/edgejwt`). An unknown or not-ready handle gets 404. The edge runs on mckean's Mac in Docker Compose behind a Cloudflare tunnel (memory mem-0khv); the map, invites and Kratos live there too.

### 2.2. The yard verifies every webhook itself, with secrets only it has

`internal/hooks/receiver.go`, `Receiver.serve`: `POST /hooks/{name}` reads the hook's secret file on every delivery (`config.ReadSecretFile`), verifies the signature through the source's adapter (`ad.Verify`), answers a verification handshake from the verified body at once (`ChallengeAnswer`, as Slack's `url_verification` needs), then normalises, dedupes (`DedupeKeys`, by the sender's delivery id) and records. A declared source can also check the sender's timestamp against the yard's clock: `internal/hooks/declared.go`, after the signature, refuses a delivery whose timestamp is further than `window` from now. Two shipped packs set it: `packs/linear/linear.yaml` (`window: 1m`) and `packs/slack/slack.yaml` (`window: 5m`). This matters for any queue in front of the yard (3.4).

### 2.3. Where a hook's URL comes from

`hook_public_url` (`internal/store/settings.go`, `HookPublicURLSetting`, checked by `CheckHookPublicURL`: https, no user, query or `..`) is the public address hooks are reached at; a hook's URL is it plus `/<name>`. Packs that register their own webhooks read it (`internal/store/runask_hook.go`, `hookPublicURL`). A hosted yard sets it to `https://my.yardr.dev/hooks/<handle>`.

### 2.4. The yard key

Every yard has an ed25519 key at `~/.yardr/peer.key` (`config.PeerKey`, made by `yardkey.Ensure` on first use; `yardr peer key` prints the public half). `internal/yardkey` signs requests (`SignRequest`: method, path, time and body hash, to a named receiver key) and answers (`SignResponse`), carried in `Yardr-Peer-Key`, `Yardr-Peer-Time` and `Yardr-Peer-Signature`, with `MaxSkew` of five minutes. Its `Invite` says "A yard without a URL cannot be called: it calls", which is the shape of a connected yard. Peers (docs/peers.md) sign everything and leave TLS to whatever stands in front.

### 2.5. Devices and the listener

`yardr user device pair <user> <device> --url <address>` (internal/cli/devicepair.go; docs/users.md, "Enrolled by a code") opens a ten-minute code the device redeems at `POST <address>/user/pair`; it refuses on a yard with no listener. The device then calls the 21 calls of docs/api.md under `/user/` with its token. `internal/rpc/user.go`, `inTheClear`: a secret (`GiveSecret`) is taken only from a loopback peer, or from the hosted router named by `router_addr`; approvals (`ApproveAsk`, `ApproveSetup`) only from loopback. The source address is `r.RemoteAddr`, never a forwarded header. footplate's hosted mode signs in with Kratos and sends both tokens (bwm2 3.6).

### 2.6. The listener is one mux, served in `yardr server`

`internal/server/server.go`, `serveHooks`: when `hook_addr` (or `YARDR_HOOK_ADDR`) is set, the server listens there and serves one `http.ServeMux` holding the hook receiver, the peers receiver and the device server. When neither is set, nothing listens. The web view is another process (`yardr web serve`, on `web_addr`, loopback unless `--unsafe`), and in JWT mode verifies the edge's token (`web_jwt_*` settings, design yardr-scpj).

### 2.7. The hosted terms and the Google app

Design bwm2's D2 (decided): hosted users bring their own harness credential, the service keeps none, and the led-login relay carries a login input in memory as a stated trial limit. Design yardr-8wxe 2.10 read Anthropic's terms on 12 Oct: a service hosting Claude Code agrees to the Commercial Terms; each end user authenticates with their own API key, subscription or third-party provider credential; advertised Pro and Max limits "assume ordinary, individual usage". The critique of this design (note 7801) read Anthropic's current Consumer Terms (https://www.anthropic.com/legal/consumer-terms, effective 2025-10-08) more narrowly: account credentials must not be shared, and automated access is allowed only through an Anthropic API key or with Anthropic's explicit permission; official support for Pro and Max in Claude Code is not, on that reading, permission for a third party's hosted agent relay. Bead yardr-hosted-4dx0 (backlog, not drafted) is a hosted Google OAuth app: a person connects Google in the browser, hosted keeps the refresh token encrypted, and a hosted mint, through the existing `mint_command` seam, serves the google packs.

## 3. The proposal

The edge that exists carries most of it: the account, the handle, the map, `/hooks/<handle>/*`, the session check on `/user/*`, footplate's hosted mode. What is new is a third kind of map row, `connected`, whose transport is a WebSocket the yard opened, instead of a TCP dial to a container or droplet; the endpoint in the router that takes that socket; a queue for deliveries that arrive while the yard is away; and on the yard's side one command and one setting that make the yard dial out and serve what arrives with the handlers it already has.

### 3.1. The picture

```text
GitHub, Linear, Slack ──POST /hooks/<handle>/<hook>──▶ my.yardr.dev router ─┬─ docker row:    http://yard-<handle>:8790   (today)
footplate ──/user/* + X-Edge-Token──────────────────▶   (session check,     ├─ droplet row:   http://<tailnet>:8790       (today)
browser ──/ + Kratos cookie─────────────────────────▶    map lookup)        └─ connected row: the yard's open WebSocket   (new)
                                                                                         ▲
                                         laptop, any network, no open port:              │ wss://my.yardr.dev/edge/link
                                         yardr server ── link ── dials out on 443 ───────┘ signed with peer.key
                                             └─ serves each request that arrives with the hook listener's own mux, in process
```

### 3.2. The link: a WebSocket the yard dials, signed with the yard key

**What carries it.** One WebSocket from `yardr server` to `wss://my.yardr.dev/edge/link`, on 443, through whatever the laptop's network allows outbound. A WebSocket and not long polling, because the edge must send requests down to the yard at any moment and get answers back (footplate's calls, later the web view), and because both Cloudflare's tunnel and Cloudflare Workers carry WebSockets natively. Each message is one JSON frame:

```text
edge → yard  {"type":"request","id":"r-81","seq":214,"method":"POST","path":"/hooks/github",
              "header":{"X-Hub-Signature-256":["sha256=…"],…},"body":"<base64, raw bytes>",
              "received":"2026-10-20T09:14:03Z","queued":false,"sig":"<edge signature>"}
yard → edge  {"type":"response","id":"r-81","status":200,"header":{…},"body":"<base64>"}
yard → edge  {"type":"ack","id":"r-81"}            (a queued delivery is done with: the edge deletes it)
yard → edge  {"type":"data","id":"r-90","body":"<base64>"}   (more of a streamed response, 3.6)
either way   {"type":"end","id":"r-90"}                       (a streamed response is over, or its caller went away)
either way   {"type":"ping"} / {"type":"pong"}
```

Requests are multiplexed by `id`, so a slow delivery does not hold up a footplate call. The body is the raw bytes the sender sent, and the headers are the sender's own, minus the hop headers and the edge's own (`X-Edge-Token`, `X-Edge-JWT`, `Cookie`), so the yard's adapter verifies the signature exactly as it does on the listener. The edge never holds a hook's signing secret and never verifies a delivery; it cannot.

**How it authenticates.** Both ends have ed25519 keys and each pins the other's at enrolment (3.5). The yard opens the socket with a `GET /edge/link` signed by `yardkey.SignRequest` to the edge's public key, the three `Yardr-Peer-*` headers as between peers, plus a header `Yardr-Link-Nonce` holding 32 random bytes the yard chose; the edge finds the `connected` row by the key, checks the signature and `MaxSkew`, and answers `101`. That signed upgrade only lets the socket open: on its own it proves nothing, because `yardkey.VerifyRequest` accepts the same recorded request again for up to `MaxSkew`, five minutes (`internal/yardkey/yardkey.go`, `MaxSkew`, "also how long a recorded request can be played again"). So the socket is not the yard's live link until a one-use challenge is answered on it:

```text
edge → yard  {"type":"challenge","nonce":"<32 random bytes of the edge's>","sig":"<edge key over both nonces>"}
yard → edge  {"type":"hello","sig":"<yard key over both nonces>"}
edge → yard  {"type":"welcome"}
```

The edge's nonce is fresh for every socket and kept only until that socket's hello, so each is answered once. The yard checks the challenge's signature against the pinned edge key before it answers (a captured edge answer cannot be played back to it, since it covers the yard's own fresh nonce), and the edge checks the hello against the row's key. Both signatures cover the two nonces under a label of their own (`yardr edge link v1`), so neither can be taken for a request signature or the other way round; that is two small functions in `internal/yardkey`, `SignLink` and `VerifyLink`, beside `SignRequest`. A socket with no valid hello within 10 seconds is closed, and until its hello it receives no frames and displaces nothing. Someone who recorded a signed upgrade and plays it back within the five minutes gets a socket and a challenge they cannot sign, and the live link stays up. The two nonces together are the link's session id: every `request` frame carries the edge's signature over the session id, its `seq`, its own id, method, path, a digest of its headers, the body hash, `received` time and `queued`, so a frame on a socket that is not the edge's is refused, a frame recorded from one link is refused on the next, and the yard can trust `received` and `queued` (3.4). `queued` is signed because it chooses which clock the yard checks the sender's timestamp against (3.4): a frame whose `queued` was flipped after signing is refused like one whose body was. `seq` is what refuses a frame played again on the same link, which the session id alone does not: the edge numbers the `request` frames of one session 1, 2, 3 and so on, in the order it sends them, and the yard refuses any frame whose `seq` is not greater than the last it accepted on that socket. A WebSocket delivers in order, so a frame from the real edge is never refused by this, and the yard keeps one number per socket instead of a set of seen ids. A `/user/call/` recorded off the wire and sent again, one that posts a message for instance, is refused the same way as a webhook. In the other direction the edge takes one `response` per id and forgets the id once it is answered, so a response played again has nothing to answer. The header digest is SHA-256 over a canonical form of the header map the frame carries: names lowercased and sorted, each line `name:value` with a name's values in the order they arrived, lines joined by newlines. It is what makes the headers as trustworthy as the body, which matters because they carry what the yard verifies with: the sender's signature, its timestamp and its delivery id, by which the yard dedupes. A frame whose headers were changed after the edge signed it is refused like one whose body was. TLS (Cloudflare's) protects the wire; the signatures say who is at each end even if a name or certificate is wrong.

**How it reconnects.** A laptop that changes network, sleeps or loses Wi-Fi often kills the socket silently: neither side sees a close. So each side sends `ping` every 30 seconds and counts the socket dead after 75 seconds with nothing heard (under the roughly 100 seconds after which Cloudflare's proxy closes an idle connection), and the yard dials again with backoff: 1 second, doubling to 60, with jitter, reset after a link that stayed up for a minute. The edge keeps one socket per yard, newest wins: a second connect from the same key that answers its challenge closes the older one, which is what a laptop that woke on a new network does. Only an answered challenge wins; a socket still waiting on its hello never closes the live one. Requests in flight on a dead socket are answered as in 3.4.

**What the yard does with a request.** It hands it to the same `http.ServeMux` `serveHooks` builds today (2.6), in process, with `RemoteAddr` set to `edge:0`, an address that is never loopback and never `router_addr`. So a hook delivery meets `Receiver.serve`, a device call meets the device server with its own token check, `inTheClear` refuses a secret or an approval that came over the link, and nothing new decides who is let in. `/peers/` is not served over the link in this design (section 4). The link needs no `hook_addr`: a laptop yard can have no listener at all and be fully reachable through the edge.

### 3.3. Webhooks: one URL per hook, the same as a hosted yard's

The URL is the one hosted yards already have: `https://my.yardr.dev/hooks/<handle>/<hook>`. Connecting sets `hook_public_url` to `https://my.yardr.dev/hooks/<handle>`, so `yardr hook add` and the packs that register their own webhooks print or register the right URL with no change. The router's `serveHooks` keeps its lookup and gains one branch: for a `connected` row it sends the request down the yard's socket instead of dialling a target.

### 3.4. A delivery when the yard is away

The edge forwards live first: with the yard connected, it sends the frame and waits up to 2 seconds for the response, which it passes back to the sender byte for byte (status, headers, body). That keeps the yard's own answers working: a Slack handshake answered from the verified body, a 401 for a bad signature, a 200 for a dropped stranger. The deadline is set by the shortest a sender allows: Slack's Events API expects a 2xx within 3 seconds and retries otherwise (https://api.slack.com/apis/connections/events-api), GitHub gives 10. What the router can promise is only the part it sees, so the design names that part and claims nothing past it. The router's clock starts when its `/hooks/` handler is entered, which is once the request's line and headers have been read from cloudflared on the same machine, and stops when the router has written its answer's last byte to cloudflared. Inside that span:

- **Reading the body: at most 0.5 seconds.** The handler sets a read deadline 0.5 seconds after entry (`http.ResponseController.SetReadDeadline`) and reads through `http.MaxBytesReader` at 1 MiB, so neither a slow sender nor a large one holds the clock. A body not read by then is answered `408` and goes nowhere; Slack, GitHub and Linear send a delivery of a few kilobytes in one burst, so this refuses only a sender that trickles.
- **The live wait: until 2 seconds after entry,** not 2 seconds after the body is read, so a slow body shortens the wait instead of adding to it.
- **The queue write and the `202`:** one SQLite insert of at most 1 MiB, milliseconds, finished by 2.5 seconds after entry.

So the claim, and what the tests of E3 and E5 measure, is that the router has written its answer within 2.5 seconds of entering the handler, whatever the yard does. The rest, Slack to Cloudflare, Cloudflare through the tunnel to cloudflared, and the answer back the same way, the router cannot see or bound. The design leaves it 0.5 seconds of Slack's 3 and does not claim Slack's 3 seconds end to end: a Cloudflare path slower than that half second turns a delivery the yard took into one Slack retries, which the yard's dedupe (2.2) then drops. Recording a delivery is a store write the yard does in milliseconds, so the live wait is spent only on a slow network.

With the yard not connected, or no answer within the 2 seconds, the edge stores the request (raw body, headers, `received`, an id) in a queue per yard and answers the sender `202` with `{"queued": "<id>"}`. GitHub does not redeliver on its own, so answering and keeping is the only way the delivery is not lost. A live frame that ran out of time is the same queued entry: if the yard's response arrives later, it counts as that entry's ack. On reconnect the edge sends the queue oldest first with `"queued": true`; the yard serves each as above and sends `ack` once it is recorded or refused, and only then does the edge delete it. A delivery sent twice (an ack lost with the socket, or a late answer and the queued copy) is caught by the yard's existing dedupe on the sender's delivery id (`DedupeKeys`, 2.2).

**What bounds the queue.** Anyone on the internet can POST to `/hooks/<handle>/<hook>`, and the edge cannot tell a real delivery from junk, since only the yard holds the secret. So every bound is checked before the edge answers `202`, and a request that would cross one is refused, never kept:

- **Body size.** The edge reads at most 1 MiB of a body, the yard's own limit (`store.MaxHookPayload`, which `Receiver.serve` applies with `http.MaxBytesReader`, `internal/hooks/receiver.go`), and answers a larger one `413` before it reaches a socket or the queue, live or away. A delivery the yard would refuse anyway is never stored. Today's router streams `/hooks/` bodies to the yard unbounded; the limit applies to every row kind, and docker and droplet rows lose nothing by it.
- **Per yard.** At most 1,000 deliveries and 64 MiB queued, and nothing kept past 7 days.
- **Across the edge.** At most 2 GiB queued for all yards together, well under the Mac's free disk, so one yard's flood or many yards' together cannot fill the disk the map and Kratos live on.

When a per-yard or edge-wide bound is reached, the edge answers the new request `503` with `X-Edge-Refusal: queue-full` and keeps what it has: refusing the newest, rather than dropping the oldest, is what stops a stranger's flood from pushing real deliveries out. GitHub marks the delivery failed in its log, Linear and Slack retry. Entries older than 7 days are dropped. Each refusal and each drop is counted on the yard's row and told to the yard on its next connect as one event (`edge_queue_refused`, with the counts), so `yardr edge status` and the crew can see it. The numbers are flags of the router.

**Junk in the queue, accepted for the trial.** The bounds cap what a stranger can cost the edge, but not what they can cost one yard: while a yard is away, anyone who knows its handle can post junk to its hook URLs until its queue is full, and its real deliveries are then refused `503` until the junk is delivered (and refused by the yard) or expires. mckean accepted this for the trial (20 Oct, ask 7807) with two guards. A Cloudflare rate-limiting rule on `my.yardr.dev/hooks/*`, set in mckean's Cloudflare account and not in code, slows any one sender; docs/edge.md's operator section gives the exact rule (bead E11a). And the router tells the operator: the first refusal for a yard, and then at most one an hour while refusals go on, runs the command named by the router's `--operator-notify` flag with the event (`edge_queue_refused`: handle, counts, the bound reached) as JSON on its standard input, the way yardr runs an operator's command for what it does rather than knowing a provider. With no command named, the router logs the same line. A hook-name list at the edge would cut most junk; it is left out (section 4).

**A verification handshake while the yard is away.** Slack sends `url_verification` when the person saves the hook's URL in Slack's app settings, and expects the challenge back in the answer. The edge never answers a handshake (it cannot verify one, section 4), so a handshake that arrives while the yard is away, or after the 2 seconds, is queued and answered `202`, and Slack shows the URL as not verified. That is a stated limit, not a failure mode to engineer around: the person saves the URL from Slack's settings with the yard connected, which is when they are setting the hook up anyway, and saves it again if Slack refuses. docs/edge.md says so.

The timestamp window (2.2) breaks this for Linear (1 minute) and Slack (5 minutes): a delivery that waited an hour fails `Verify` on arrival. So for a frame with `"queued": true` and a valid edge signature, the yard checks the sender's timestamp against the edge-signed `received` time instead of its own clock. The window keeps its job, refusing a capture replayed later, at the edge's door instead of the yard's: the edge could replay a captured delivery with a fresh `received`, which is said as a limit, since the edge is ours and holds the payloads anyway (3.7). Decided (section 5, question 2).

### 3.5. Connecting a yard: one command, approved in the browser

The account is the hosted one: a Kratos identity with a verified email and a handle (bwm2 3.2). On the yard:

```console
$ yardr edge connect https://my.yardr.dev
The edge's key: ed25519:9bR…T1  (fetched over HTTPS from my.yardr.dev; also on https://yardr.dev/edge)
This yard's key: ed25519:Hk3…q9
Open https://my.yardr.dev/connect and enter the code  WQ4F-7K2M  (10 minutes)
… approved by chris@mckean.co as mckean
Connected: webhooks reach this yard at https://my.yardr.dev/hooks/mckean/<hook>
hook_public_url set to https://my.yardr.dev/hooks/mckean
The link is up (yardr edge status).
```

**The first trust step.** The yard has no edge key yet, and `yardkey.SignRequest` signs a request to a named receiver key (`internal/yardkey/yardkey.go`: the signature covers `to`), so the key must come first. The command fetches it with `GET <url>/edge/key` over HTTPS, with Go's default certificate checks against the system's roots, and refuses a plain `http` URL except on loopback (for tests). It prints the key, pins it in memory for the rest of the enrolment, and writes it into the `edge` setting only when the enrolment completes. The trust assumption, stated: whoever holds a valid certificate for my.yardr.dev at the moment of connecting is the edge. Today that is Cloudflare, which ends TLS in front of the tunnel, and us behind it. After that moment the pin holds on its own: a later change of certificate or of DNS, or a different key served at `/edge/key`, does not move it, and the link refuses an edge that cannot sign with the pinned key. A person who does not want to take the certificate's word can compare the printed key with the one published at https://yardr.dev/edge, a page outside the edge's own hosting. This is trust on first use, as `ssh` does with a host key, with the web's certificates making the first use hard to intercept.

The command then posts the yard's public key and its name, signed to the pinned edge key, to `/edge/enrol`; the edge answers with a code, its answer signed with `yardkey.SignResponse`, and the command refuses an answer that the pinned key did not sign; the person, signed in at `/connect`, types the code and sees the yard's name and the start of its key before approving; the command, waiting on the same signed call, gets the handle back. It writes the setting `edge` (the edge's URL, its public key and the handle, one value) and `hook_public_url`, and the running server dials. `hook_public_url` is a setting the operator may already have set by hand, to a tunnel of their own for example (`internal/store/settings.go`, `HookPublicURLSetting`). So connect keeps the value it replaces inside the `edge` setting, says so (`hook_public_url was https://hooks.example.net; restored on disconnect`), and `yardr edge disconnect` puts that value back, or clears the setting when there was none. The edge writes the row through the provisioner's `register`, as for any yard: backend `connected`, target empty, the yard's public key, state `ready`. This is the device-code shape every TV app uses, and it means no token is ever pasted or kept on the yard: the yard key is its credential.

**Who may connect, and choosing hosted or connected.** Connecting takes the same invite as a hosted yard: `/connect` refuses an account whose email is not on the trial's invite list, as `/ready` does today (`yardr-hosted/router/internal/router/ready.go`). An account chooses once, for now, between a hosted yard and a connected one. Today `/ready` provisions a hosted yard for every verified invited account; it gains the choice first, a page that asks "a yard we run for you" or "connect your own yard", and only the first goes on to provisioning. `/connect` refuses an account that already has a hosted yard, and `/ready` refuses to provision for one that already has a connected yard, each with a sentence saying why and that the other must be deleted first. mckean's view (20 Oct, ask 7808) is that the two should not stand in each other's way, and that one account could later have a connected laptop yard and a hosted one. So the connected row, its queue and its enrolment are keyed by the handle, the yard, never by the account; one yard per account is a single check, in one function both pages call, and a second yard later lifts that check instead of reshaping the map.

**The check is atomic per account.** Two requests for one account can come at once, from two tabs or a script: `/ready` choosing hosted and `/connect` approving a yard, each for a different handle. The provisioner's lock is per handle (`provision_locks` and `acquire_lock` in `yardr-hosted/provision/provision`), so both would pass a check made under it and the account would end with two yards. So the check runs under a lock per account as well: a table `account_locks`, keyed by the account's Kratos identity id (`kratos_id` on the map's rows), of the same shape as `provision_locks` and taken and reclaimed the same way, with its dead-owner rule. Both paths go through the provisioner for this: `/connect`'s approval calls `provision register --backend connected`, and `/ready`'s hosted choice is the provision trigger it already sends. Each takes the account's lock first and the handle's second, always in that order so two of them never wait on each other; under both it counts the account's rows that have been chosen (a `connected` row, or a hosted row past `pending`), refuses with the sentence above if there is one, and otherwise writes its own row's choice (the `connected` row, or the hosted row moved out of `pending`) before it lets the account's lock go. A row that is only registered, which every verified sign-up has, is not a yard and does not count. Of two such requests at once, exactly one makes a yard and the other is refused.

**Switching, in the trial.** No page switches an account from a hosted yard to a connected one. A person who wants to asks the operator, who deletes the hosted yard with the existing `provision delete <handle>` (the container and network go, the volume is kept, as for any delete), after which the person connects as above. The other way, from connected to hosted, is `yardr edge disconnect` on their own machine, which drops the row, and then the hosted choice at `/ready`. A self-serve switch is left out (section 4).

```console
$ yardr edge status

link      up since 09:12 (3 reconnects today; last: network changed at 08:47)
```

`yardr status` and `yardr doctor` show the link's line beside the listener's; the server logs `edge_link_up` and `edge_link_down` events (with the reason: closed, unanswered pings, refused key) so a crew member can be told.

### 3.6. footplate and the web view through the same link

**footplate, in the first slice after webhooks.** Nothing changes in footplate: its hosted mode already signs in with Kratos and sends `X-Edge-Token` beside the device token (bwm2 3.6). The router's session half gains the same `connected` branch: `/user/*` from a session whose row is `connected` goes down that row's socket.

The 2-second live wait and the queue of 3.4 are for webhooks alone: they exist because Slack answers its own deadline and GitHub does not redeliver, and neither is true of footplate. A device call waits for the yard's answer up to 30 seconds, footplate's own limit on a call (`CALL_TIMEOUT` in footplate's `src/api.rs`), so the edge never gives up on a slow but healthy yard before footplate would; past that, or with no socket, the router answers `504` or `503` with `X-Edge-Refusal: away`, and nothing is queued (section 4). The one call that is not a call is `GET /user/events`, the stream footplate follows (`internal/rpc/user.go`, `events`), which stays open and silent while nothing happens. It has no deadline at the edge. The yard answers it with a `response` frame that carries the status and headers, then one `data` frame for each piece it writes and flushes, and `end` when the stream closes; the router writes and flushes each piece to footplate as it comes. When footplate goes away, the router sends `end` with that id, and the yard cancels the request's context, which ends the stream there as a closed connection does on the listener. The link's pings keep the socket alive under a silent stream, and footplate's own keepalive keeps its connection to Cloudflare alive. A link that drops ends every stream on it, and footplate opens its stream again with `after=<last seen>`, as it does today when its network changes. `yardr user device pair <user> footplate` counts an up link as a listener and takes the edge's address as the default `--url` (`https://my.yardr.dev`), so pairing is pairing by code as today. Two gates, as for a hosted yard: the edge's (the account's session) and the yard's (the device token). Over the link, `inTheClear` refuses `GiveSecret` and the approvals (3.2); the person answers those at the yard's own machine, as they would on a LAN without TLS. A connected yard takes no secrets over the link (decided, section 5, question 4).

**The web view: later, a change of scope said as one.** The bead asks for footplate and the web view through the same connection. This design carries footplate through the server's link and leaves the web view out for connected yards until footplate over the link is in use (decided, section 5, question 6; section 4). The reason it cannot simply ride along: the view is another process, `yardr web serve` (`internal/cli/web.go`), which builds `web.NewServer(st, home, log)` over the rpc client and listens on loopback at `web_addr` and at `pages_addr` for the sandbox origin where a note's pages run (2.6). The server's link serves the listener's mux in process, so it cannot reach that other process without forwarding to a loopback port, which is a proxy inside the yard and ruled out by mem-stdz. The two ways that remain for later, `yardr server` serving the view over its own `*Store` on its one link, or `yardr web serve` opening a second link of its own, are kept in section 5 for whoever takes it up.

### 3.7. What the edge can read, and what it never holds

Said on the connect page and in docs/edge.md, plainly:

- **It can read** every webhook payload and its headers (TLS ends at Cloudflare and at the router); every footplate call and answer: conversations, beads, mail, asks, notes; later every web-view page. The queue keeps webhook payloads at rest on the edge for up to the queue's bound. Nothing through the edge is end-to-end encrypted; the yard-key signatures prove who sent a frame, they hide nothing.
- **It never holds** repositories (git runs from the yard to GitHub directly, never through the link); hook signing secrets (they are in the yard's secret files, and the yard verifies); the yard's private key; model credentials of a connected yard (the harness logs in on the person's machine, and secrets are refused over the link). For a hosted yard, bwm2 D2 stands as decided: the service keeps no harness credential, and the led-login relay carries a login input through it in memory, stored nowhere.
- **It holds by the person's choice** the Google refresh token, when they connect Google through yardr-hosted-4dx0's app. That is the one credential the edge keeps, encrypted, revocable by the person, and the reason a yard would use the hosted mint at all.

### 3.8. Hosted and connected: what they share and where they differ

| | hosted yard | connected yard |
|---|---|---|
| account, handle, sign-up | Kratos, the same pages | the same |
| map row | `docker` or `droplet`, made by the provisioner | `connected`, made by `yardr edge connect` |
| how the edge reaches it | TCP to the container or droplet | the WebSocket the yard opened |
| webhook URL | `https://my.yardr.dev/hooks/<handle>/<hook>` | the same |
| footplate | Kratos session plus device token, pairing by code | the same |
| web view | JWT from the router | none yet (3.6) |
| yard key on the map row | recorded by the provisioner when the yard is built (below) | given at enrolment (3.5) |
| who runs it, upgrades, backs up | the service | the person |
| harness credential | the person's, by any login method; they state that their provider's terms allow it (below) | the person's, on their own machine; the service is not running it |
| secrets over the edge | from the router (`router_addr`) | refused over the link |
| Google | the hosted mint (4dx0) | the hosted mint, if they connect Google |
| invite list | yes, for the trial | yes, the same list |
| choosing between them | one yard per account for now, chosen once at sign-up (3.5) | the same |

**The hosted limit and how sign-up checks it.** The strategy's line is that a hosted yard runs on API keys, or provider plans whose terms allow hosted automated use. mckean decided (20 Oct, ask 7804) that whether a credential is allowed is the person's to judge and not the service's: "this can be offloaded to the user, they may use api keys, they may use subscriptions. For us this is a non topic." So sign-up checks it by a statement alone. The page that offers a hosted yard says that the yard runs agents with the person's own harness credential, that they are responsible for their provider's terms allowing that use, and that an API key is the way those terms most plainly allow; a hosted yard is made only after the person ticks it. There is no list of allowed methods: in the yard, the hosted yardmaster's role, which leads the login relay (bwm2 3.5), offers every login method its harness kind has. Nothing reads or tests the key: probing it would be handling it, which D2 rules out. The known risk, which mckean accepted with the decision: on the critique's reading of Anthropic's Consumer Terms (2.7), a person who signs a hosted yard in with a Claude Pro or Max subscription may be outside those terms, and a hosted service that offers that login invites it; the statement puts the choice with the person, and does not make it allowed.

**Google (yardr-hosted-4dx0).** The hosted mint takes yard-key-signed calls: a yard, hosted or connected, runs the google pack's `mint_command` as a small command that calls `POST https://my.yardr.dev/mint/google` signed with `peer.key`, and the edge finds the account by the key and answers with a short-lived access token. That needs every yard's public key on its map row, and today no row has one: `edgemap.Row` (`yardr-hosted/router/internal/edgemap/map.go`) and the `yards` table `init_db` makes in `yardr-hosted/provision/provision` hold handle, email, identity, backend, target, volume, network, state, error and the pairing code, and no key. A connected yard gives its key at enrolment (3.5), which writes it into a new `yard_key` column. A hosted yard never enrols, so the provisioner records it instead: once the yard is built, the provisioner already runs yardr inside it (`in_yard` through `docker exec` for docker, `as_yard` over ssh for a droplet, as it does to set the hosted settings), and it runs `yardr peer key` the same way and writes the answer into `yard_key` through `register`. `provision join`, with no handle, fills the column for every ready row that lacks it, so the yards built before this change get one at the edge's next start. A yard whose key changes (a restore onto a fresh home) gets the new key written by the same step on its rebuild. So the mint finds the account by key for both kinds of yard, with no second token. This design fixes that contract and the key binding (beads E1 and E8); 4dx0 designs the app and the mint itself.

### 3.9. Where the edge's side runs: Go, in the router

The router already does the edge's whole job for two backends: the session check, the map, `/hooks/<handle>`, the JWT, footplate's refusal shapes. The link is a third backend of the same router, in Go, with the queue in a SQLite file beside the map (the router's own, written by the router, since the queue is the router's state and not the provisioner's). Signatures come from the same ed25519 code yardr uses, copied or imported as a small module. mckean's TypeScript idea fits Cloudflare Workers with a Durable Object per yard (socket and queue in one object, nothing for us to keep running), and it is the stronger choice once the edge leaves the Mac; but it would split one edge into two services that must agree on accounts, sessions and the map. Decided (section 5, question 1): Go, in the router. It must still be movable off the Mac later, to be hosted properly: the link backend and its queue take their paths and addresses from the router's flags and configuration, assume nothing about the Mac, Docker Compose or the tunnel, and the frame protocol stays exactly as 3.2 has it, so the backend can be moved to another machine, or ported to Workers, without the yard noticing.

### 3.10. The line mem-stdz draws, kept

The yard gains no listener, no TLS, no proxy rules and no knowledge of tunnels or networks. It gains one outbound setting, `edge`, naming one service by URL and key, which it dials and nothing else; requests that come down the link are served by the handlers the listener already has, in process, never forwarded to another address or port. What is allowed is still said by the yard's own rules: hooks by their secrets, devices by their tokens, secrets and approvals only from loopback. Everything that is relay, queue, TLS and routing lives in a service we run, outside yardr.

### 3.11. The first slice

One connected yard, mckean's laptop yard, webhooks only, with the queue: E1 to E6 of section 6. A laptop is away often, and GitHub does not redeliver on its own, so a slice without the queue would lose every delivery sent while the laptop sleeps or changes network; that is not a usable slice, and the queue is in it.

The slice is done when, with the laptop on a network with no open port:

- a GitHub push to one of his repositories reaches his laptop yard live through `https://my.yardr.dev/hooks/mckean/github`;
- it keeps arriving after he moves the laptop from home Wi-Fi to a phone hotspot;
- a push made while the laptop is asleep, answered `202` by the edge, is recorded by the yard within a minute of waking, once, and is gone from the edge's queue after the yard's ack;
- a Linear delivery queued for ten minutes verifies on arrival.

On the way, E1 to E4 together are a checkpoint for builders and not a slice anyone is offered: there, a delivery to an away yard gets `503` with `X-Edge-Refusal: away` and is lost to the yard. GitHub keeps it in the hook's delivery log, marked failed, where it can be redelivered by hand, and Linear and Slack say in their documentation that they retry a failed delivery a few times; nothing in yardr gets it back. mckean's laptop yard is not pointed at the edge until E5 lands and the Cloudflare rule of 3.4 is set. Footplate through the link (E7a and E7b) follows the slice.

## 4. Left out

- **Billing, teams and Junction.** Left out by the bead. The `connected` row is where a paid flag would sit later.
- **The web view for connected yards.** Decided later (section 5, question 6): no web view through the link until footplate through it is in use, which will show whether the browser view is wanted. This narrows the bead's ask, as 3.6 says; the bead it would have been (E10 in earlier drafts) is not in section 6.
- **Peers through the link.** Two connected yards could link through the edge (`/peers/` frames), but peers today are the operator's own networks with signatures end to end; routing them through our service changes who can see their mail, and nobody asked for it.
- **End-to-end encryption between footplate and the yard.** Possible with a key footplate and the yard share at pairing, so the edge carries only ciphertext for `/user/*`, but webhooks cannot be (the sender encrypts for TLS only), and footplate's hosted mode would change. The bead asks to say what the edge reads, not to hide it.
- **A queue for footplate calls.** A device call to an away yard gets `503` with `X-Edge-Refusal: away`; only webhooks are kept, since only their senders do not try again.
- **Yards on several accounts, or several yards on one account.** One account, one yard, one handle, as hosted v0, chosen once (3.5). Since the connected row, queue and enrolment are keyed by handle, a second yard per account later, a hosted one beside a connected laptop yard for instance, lifts one check rather than reshaping the map.
- **A self-serve switch between hosted and connected.** Connecting stops nobody's hosted yard; it is refused while one exists, and in the trial the operator deletes a hosted yard on request (3.5). A page that switches, stopping the hosted yard and keeping its volume for export, waits until someone asks.
- **Queueing only for hook names the yard has.** The yard could tell the edge its hook names in its hello, so the edge refuses `/hooks/<handle>/<unknown>` instead of queueing it. The byte and count bounds of 3.4 cap what a stranger can cost the edge, and junk filling one away yard's queue is accepted for the trial with the guards of 3.4; the name list is the tightening to make before strangers connect, and it would put a little of the yard's configuration at the edge.
- **The edge running anything for the yard.** It answers no handshake, verifies nothing, and never writes to a yard except through the frames above.

## 5. Decided

The draft's six open questions and two that its critique raised (note 7801) were put to mckean as asks 7802 to 7809 and answered on 20 Oct, 08:25 to 08:33. The options each was chosen from are recorded on the asks; the answers are:

1. **Where the edge's half of the link runs** (ask 7802): Go, in `yardr-hosted/router`, as a third backend beside docker and droplet, its queue in a SQLite file of the router's. mckean: "this can still be moved off of the mac at some point so we can have this properly hosted later somewhere." So paths and addresses come from configuration, nothing assumes the Mac or the tunnel, and the frame protocol of 3.2 is kept as written, so the backend can move or be ported to Workers later (3.9).
2. **The sender's timestamp on a queued delivery** (ask 7803): the yard checks it against the edge-signed `received` time, so queued Linear and Slack deliveries verify, trusting the edge not to replay (3.4). E6 is in the first slice.
3. **The hosted credential limit** (ask 7804): no list, a statement at sign-up; the person may use API keys or subscriptions, and the relay offers every login method its harness has. There is no `credentials.yaml`. mckean accepted the known risk of the critique's Consumer Terms reading (2.7, 3.8).
4. **Secrets over the link to a connected yard** (ask 7805): no. Secrets and approvals are answered on the yard's own machine, and the edge never carries a credential of a connected yard (3.6, 3.7).
5. **Who may connect a yard** (ask 7806): the trial's invite list, the same as hosted, until there is a cost guard per account (3.5).
6. **The web view for a connected yard** (ask 7809): later, once footplate through the link is in use (3.6, section 4). When it is taken up, the two ways open are `yardr server` serving `web.NewServer` over its own `*Store` on its one link, frames routed by an `origin` the edge sets from the host (one socket, as the bead asked, with a page handler's fault becoming the server's), or `yardr web serve` opening a second link with the same key and enrolment (two sockets per yard). A proxy to the view's loopback port is not one, by mem-stdz.
7. **Junk filling an away yard's queue** (ask 7807): accepted for the trial, with a Cloudflare rate limit on `/hooks/` and an event to the operator when a yard's queue refuses (3.4).
8. **Choosing hosted or connected** (ask 7808): choose once, for now; connecting is refused while a hosted yard exists and the other way round. mckean: "you could have a connected yard to your laptop and then also get a hosted one. I guess for now it doesn't matter and we can go with choose once. But I think this shouldn't be in the way of one another." So the connected row, queue and enrolment are keyed by handle, and one yard per account is one check (3.5).

Taken into the beads without an ask, from the critique's build-plan items (yardmaster, 20 Oct 08:10): `hook_public_url` kept and restored on disconnect (3.5, E2); a canonical header digest in the frame signature (3.2, E3, E4); Slack's 3 seconds measured from ingress to the answer (3.4, E3, E5); the docs and the published key split by depot (E11a, E11b). And from its second critique (note 7836, yardmaster note 7837): `queued` and a per-session `seq` in the frame signature, so a flipped delivery mode or a frame played again on the same link is refused (3.2, E3, E4); device calls with their own 30-second wait and the event stream carried as a stream, the 2-second wait being for webhooks alone (3.6, E7a); the one-yard check made under a lock per account (3.5, E1); the timing boundary said as the router's handler and its answer, with the body read bounded and no claim past the router (3.4, E3, E5); the connect page's link to docs/edge.md in a bead of its own (E12); and, decided by the yardmaster, no self-serve switch in the trial: the operator deletes a hosted yard on request (3.5, section 4).
