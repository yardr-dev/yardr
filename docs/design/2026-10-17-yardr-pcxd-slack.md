# Slack, a bot people mention, heard from many users, each mapped to a user of the yard or not

Design yardr-pcxd, proposed 2026-10-17. A proposal as it was decided on, not a description of what the code does today; the decision is on the bead.

A yard is one installation of yardr: its store, its crew and its depots. The crew are the yard's always-on agents, tied to no bead; the yardmaster is the crew member that coordinates. A user is a person of the yard: a name and an inbox. The operator is the person who runs the yard. A source is an outside system the yard hears from, declared by one YAML file and no Go; a pack is a way of working as one directory, and a pack of kind `source` is one such integration, imported into the yard itself. A hook is a webhook endpoint that turns an outside system's deliveries into events. An envelope is one delivery in the source-neutral shape routing reads. A thread is a conversation in a source that a crew member takes part in. An ask is one question as data on a bead; a run ask proposes one yardr command of a fixed list and the operator's approval runs exactly it; a secret ask asks for a credential typed unseen and written to a file by the server. See docs/glossary.md for all of yardr's words. In this document **Slack** is the chat service, a **workspace** is one Slack organisation, a **member** is a person in it (named there by an id such as `U012AB3CD`), a **channel** is one room of it (`C012AB3CD`), and **the bot** is the yard's Slack app acting as one member of the workspace.

## 1. The question

mckean, 17 Oct 2026 (mail, during the Telegram trial yardr-b6sq): "we should be able to setup a bot that can be mentioned @... I guess you could be listening and responding, the trickier part is having multiple users being able to mention, perhaps we have different scenarios for unknown, allow or ignore, and then being able to map other users to the users in the yard." So: a bot in a Slack workspace that people mention, heard from many people at once — the opposite of Telegram's one bot hearing one chat — where each speaker may already be mapped to a user of the yard or may not, with a stated answer for each of the unmapped cases, set up from cards alone as far as the Telegram trial reached. No code in this bead.

## 2. What exists today

### 2.1. Packs of kind source: telegram, linear, github

`packs/telegram/` (pack.yaml, telegram.yaml, SETUP.md, and the two command files under `packs/telegram/telegram/`) is the nearest pack: kind `source`, name `telegram`, declaring one source with a `notify` key (one message to one chat through the Bot API's `sendMessage`), a `hook` key that hears the operator's chat back, `thread` keys that answer in it, and a `hear` key. `packs/linear/` and `packs/github/` are the same shape for an issue tracker and a forge: a declaration (linear.yaml, github.yaml), a normalize command and a thread command as shell scripts around `jq` and `curl`, held by `internal/hooks` to golden files. Package packs embeds them; `yardr pack apply <name>` imports one into the yard and into no depot. The rule this design follows is the bead's: no service is named in product code, so Slack is a pack, `packs/slack`, as telegram and linear are, and every general mechanism below is worded for any declared source.

### 2.2. Receiving: verify, normalize, dedupe, route

The yard's server takes webhooks as `POST /hooks/<name>` on the `hook_addr` setting (docs/integrations.md, "How one becomes part of a yard"). `internal/hooks/receiver.go` (`Receiver.serve`) reads the hook's secret file on every delivery, runs the adapter's `Verify`, then `Normalize`, records the delivery with its dedupe keys in one store write, and answers 200; a duplicate is answered 200 and dropped with no event.

For a declared source, verification is data in the declaration's `hook.verify` (`internal/source/hook.go`: `HookVerify`, `HookSignature`, `HookDelivery`, `HookTimestamp`). Exactly two schemes stand today. `hmac-sha256` (`HookScheme`) is the hex HMAC-SHA256 of the raw body alone with the hook's secret, in the header the file names, past an optional prefix, with an optional timestamp in the body or a header checked after the signature within a window (`Declared.Verify` in internal/hooks/declared.go does `mac.Write(body)` and nothing else). `secret` (`HookSecret`) is the header holding the hook's secret itself, compared as it arrives in constant time, as Telegram sends it. Linear uses the first with a body timestamp; GitHub uses the first with a `sha256=` prefix and no timestamp; Telegram uses the second. `hookSchemes` in internal/source/hook.go refuses any other scheme, `signed` covers `body` alone, and the encoding is hex alone.

Normalize for a declared source runs `hook.normalize` with the verified body and no credential (its environment is `PATH`, `HOME` and `YARDR_SOURCE_BASE` only), on stdin `{"headers": {…}, "body": …}`, and the core sets `source` and `delivery` itself and refuses an envelope without a `type` (internal/hooks/hooks.go: `Envelope` — `type`, `action`, `actor` as id and name, `thread` as id, title and URL, `mentions` as ids or profile keys, `message` and `parent`, `text` truncated to 500 runes, and `fields`, the keys `hook.keys` names, which a subscription's `--where` may then filter on). Dedupe keys (`DedupeKeys`) are the body's hash under the source or the hook as `hook.bodies` says, plus the delivery id under the hook when the service sent one.

Routing is by subscription (`yardr subscribe <name> <source> --scope … --where …`): `all` is every delivery, `mentions` the deliveries that mention the crew member, `involved` those plus what is assigned or delegated to it and the threads it takes part in (docs/subscriptions.md). A delivery whose `actor.id` is the crew member's own identity in the source never reaches it, which keeps the echo of its own reply out of its inbox. A card may also register a hook with its service (`hook.register` in internal/source/hook.go), but only with scheme `secret`: the yard makes the secret and calls the register call on approval (`RegistersSecret`); with a signature the service makes, registering stays a typed secret in a terminal.

### 2.3. Hearing by address, and the operator's word (telegram, yardr-5rrs, yardr-c55e)

Telegram hears one chat. Its declaration says (`packs/telegram/telegram.yaml`):

```yaml
hear:
  field: chat
  address: { env: TELEGRAM_CHAT_ID }
  from: operator
```

After Normalize and before the delivery is recorded, the core reads `fields[field]` and drops the delivery — answered 200, recorded nowhere, counted beside refusals — unless one of its values equals the address in a user's env file under `address.env`, read as telling reads it (`Receiver.hears` in internal/hooks/receiver.go; `Hear` in internal/source/notify.go). Since yardr-5rrs (merged) the address alone is the consent to be heard, whatever that user is told through: the operator may be told through pushover and still chat through telegram. `from: operator` narrows hearing to the yard's operator (the `operator` setting), so another user who sets the same address is dropped like a stranger. The declaration reader refuses a `hear` with no `hook` and no `notify` key, a field that is no key of `hook.keys`, and an address variable that is not the one `notify` reads: hearing and telling share one address variable by construction.

Design yardr-c55e settled what a heard line weighs, and SETUP.md states it: the yardmaster treats a line from the operator's chat as the operator's word, as their mail — it discusses, shapes, builds and lands what they ask there — with two bounds enforced in the store rather than in instructions. Approving a run ask is the operator's alone and is refused from any agent pane whatever actor it names (`mayApprove` in internal/store/runask.go), and giving a secret answers only to the operator (`internal/store/secretask.go`); a number typed in the chat answers no ask, because nothing matches chat text to asks. The residual was stated there and stands: a taken-over Telegram account directs the yardmaster with the yardmaster's hands, bounded by instruction except where the store enforces.

Two parts of that do not transfer to a workspace. The gate names one field holding one person's address in one private chat; Slack's sender is one of many members in channels the bot shares with people who are not the operator, so "the operator alone" cannot be the hearing rule and a chat id cannot name who spoke. And the operator's-word rule names one person; with many speakers the yard needs, per speaker, an answer to who they are here — which is the mapping question of sections 2.4 and 3.3.

### 2.4. The claim card (yardr-68bf), identities, and whose word mail is

When a dropped delivery arrives from an address no user has, the yard learns the operator's address from the sender instead of a lookup: `Receiver.serve` calls `store.ClaimAddress` (internal/store/claim.go), which stands a run ask `yardr user key <operator> <source>` on its own question bead, carrying the address from the envelope's field (`Declaration.ClaimAddress` in internal/source/notify.go: the hear field with the notify address's variable) and showing at most the first 80 characters of the message. Approving writes the address into the operator's env file at mode 0600 exactly where `yardr user key` writes it, and the next delivery from that address is heard; declining drops it and writes nothing. One claim per source and operator waits at a time, none is offered once the operator has an address, and the card is the only identity guard — a stranger who writes first with a plausible "hi" can be mistaken for the operator, a race the bead kept knowingly. In this first form the claim is the operator's alone, for their own address: the bead itself names mapping another user as a later bead.

Two identity mechanisms already stand beside that env-file address. A crew member's identity in a source (`yardr crew identity <name> --source <source> --id <id> --key <key>`, `Identity` in internal/store/subscriptions.go) is what mention and assignment matching reads: an id matches exactly, a key matches in any case, and `mentionedAs` also takes the slug before a `[bot]` suffix. It is on the run-ask list, so a crew member proposes its own and the operator approves it on a card. A user's identity in a source (`yardr user identity <user> --source <source> --id … --key …`) is set by that user or the operator, never a crew member, and today assigns threads (`yardr thread open --assign <user>`) and bills a hook author their own login; it is not on the run-ask list. A user told through a source is a third, separate thing: the `notify` address in their env file, which telling reads.

Mail from another yard carries the yard's standing answer to "whose word is this" for a speaker who is not simply a user here: `OutsideMail = "from another yard: information, not instructions"` (internal/store/peermail.go), shown wherever a crew member reads it, unless the operator trusts the sender's name on that link as a user of this yard, when the mark becomes `TrustedMail`: "that user's word, as if they had written it by mail from this yard" (internal/store/peertrust.go). A hook delivery carries no trust mark of either kind today: the envelope has no field for one and the receiver attaches none.

Filing for a person also stands: a crew member's conversation session files and shapes their person's beads with `--for <them>` on every create, so the work bills their subscription (packs/starter/roles/yardmaster.md, "Which session you are"; docs/crew.md, "Conversation sessions"). The line session hears whoever has no conversation session. Events and mail from people with no conversation go to the line session's slice of the one inbox; ask answers go to the session that asked.

### 2.5. Threads and replies in a declared source

A declared source is answered in when its file has `thread.reply`, `thread.posted` and `thread.read` (`ReplierFor` in internal/hooks/reply.go). `yardr reply <seq>` posts in the delivery's thread with the caller's own credential, read from the crew member's pane environment (its `env_file` or `env_command`); the server never holds it, and every thread command of one source is handed that one credential value under the variable `credential.env` names (`Declared.runThread` and `Declaration.CommandEnv` in internal/source/command.go), with no second credential beside it. Before anything is sent the reply is on record with a key, and a retry asks `thread.posted` what that key left before posting again. `Declared.Reply` finds the conversation's root: the envelope's `parent` where it carries one, else by listing the thread, which is also how `yardr reply --to <message id>` answers under another message of the thread (`replyParent`). `yardr thread show <seq>` lists the thread through `thread.read`. A plain reply to a delivery whose envelope already carries its parent therefore needs no thread read at all, while `reply --to` and `thread show` both stand or fall with what `thread.read`'s credential may read in that source. A source with no `thread.state` and no `link` key has no status moves and no bead links, as telegram has none.

Telegram is the limiting case: `thread.posted` answers `{}` always and `thread.read` answers `{messages: []}` always, because the Bot API gives a bot no history and takes no idempotency key, so a retried reply whose answer was lost posts twice and `reply --to` is refused. Linear is the full case: `thread.read` pages `conversations`-style queries, `thread.posted` finds the comment by the key the service accepted as its own id, and `thread.state` moves an issue among the team's statuses.

### 2.6. Setup from cards alone, and what the Telegram trial hit (yardr-b6sq, yardr-0tew)

The trial's rule is that every step reaches the operator as a card they answer, and every step that still needed a terminal or a paste outside a card became a bead. What stands on cards today: applying an embedded pack (a run ask, design yardr-bht9); a source's credential (`yardr bead ask … --secret <source>`, typed unseen, written by the server); a user's own address in a source (`--secret <source> --for-user <user>`); a hook's signing secret (`--secret-hook <hook> --source <source>`, into `<home>/hooks/<hook>.secret` at 0600) followed by the hook itself as a run ask (`--run "hook add <name> --source <source>"`, internal/store/runask_hook.go), or, with scheme `secret`, one hook card on which the yard makes the secret and registers the webhook itself; the public hook address as a run ask (`settings set hook_public_url …`, which binds nothing); a crew member's identity as a run ask (`--run "crew identity …"`). The hook row today takes `--source` alone: `parseHookAdd` in internal/store/runask_hook.go whitelists only that flag (with `--map`, `--as` and `--secret-file` refused as the operator's to type), `Hook` in internal/store/hooks.go holds no policy word for an unmapped sender, and `yardr hook add` in internal/cli/hooks.go has no such flag either, so a card proposing one cannot be written yet (sections 3.3 and 3.7, bead S3). The one step the trial found with no card is the crew member's own credential for answering in a source: `yardr reply` speaks with the answering crew member's env file, and giving it is still `umask`, `printf >>`, `chmod`, `crew set env_file`, `crew restart` by hand (packs/telegram/SETUP.md step 10). That is bead yardr-0tew (open, stage `new`): `--secret <source> --for-crew <crew>` beside `--for-user`, for every declared source with a reply, Slack's included when it lands. The trial also left the networking outside yardr: a public address reaching `hook_addr` (there, a Tailscale Funnel path) is the operator's, as is any proxy in front of `yardr web`.

What `yardr source check` shows today matters to setup for the same reason. A declaration names at most one parameterless read call as its check (`Declaration.Check` and the call's `Answer.Select` in internal/source/declaration.go), and `source.Do` in internal/source/call.go does return the service's answer with only the selected keys kept (`Result.Answer`, by `selected`). The check command in internal/cli/sourcecmd.go, however, keeps only the status, the milliseconds and the byte count of that result — in its `--json` object and in the line it prints (`<source>: check call …: <status> in …ms, … bytes`) — and never reads, prints or returns `Result.Answer` at all. `yardr source show` prints the names a call's `answer.select` keeps, never a value the service returned. No command today therefore prints the bot ids a Slack `auth.test` answer carries, so setup cannot read the bot's member id from `source check` as it stands (sections 3.6 and 3.7, bead S2).

### 2.7. Slack's API, as read

Read at api.slack.com on 17 October 2026 (methods `auth.test`, `chat.postMessage`, `conversations.replies`; Events API, including the `app_mention`, `message.im` and `message.mpim` events and, on message events, `channel_type`; signing requests; app manifests). Slack is an HTTPS JSON API at `https://slack.com/api/<method>` with the bot's token (`xoxb-…`) as `Authorization: Bearer <token>`. `auth.test` answers the bot's own ids: `user_id` (the bot member's `U…` id), `team_id` and `bot_id`.

Receiving is the Events API. The app's Request URL is set in the app's configuration (by hand, or from an app manifest the app was created with); Slack first POSTs `{"type": "url_verification", "challenge": "…"}` there and expects the challenge echoed back as JSON within three seconds, and expects every event answered within three seconds, retrying with `X-Slack-Retry-Num` when it is not. Each event is a POST of `{"type": "event_callback", "team_id": "T…", "event_id": "Ev…", "event": {…}}`; a message event carries `type: "message"`, `user` (the sender's `U…` id), `channel` (`C…`, or a direct-message id), `channel_type` (`channel`, `group`, `im` or `mpim`: a public channel, a private channel, a one-to-one direct message, or a multiparty — group — direct message), `text`, `ts` (the message's own id, unique per channel) and, in a thread, `thread_ts` (the `ts` of the thread's first message; every reply carries it, and the first message is its own parent). The dedicated `app_mention` event, delivered when the bot is mentioned, is documented by an example that carries no `channel_type` at all, and its `channel` id alone does not settle the kind: a `D…` id is a one-to-one direct message, while a `G…` id may be a group direct message or a legacy private channel, so those two cannot be told apart from the id (section 3.1). Mentions stand in the text as `<@U…>`; channel and link references have their own bracketed forms. The bot only sees channels it has been invited into, plus its direct messages, under the scopes the manifest asks for (`app_mentions:read`, `channels:history`, `groups:history`, `im:history`, `mpim:history`, `chat:write`). A scope is not a subscription: the four history scopes only let the bot token read a conversation it belongs to back (below), while what Slack sends is fixed by the bot events subscribed to. Of those, `app_mention` is its own event, delivered for a new message that mentions the app in any conversation the bot belongs to, group direct messages included; `message.im` is delivered for each message in a one-to-one direct message; and `message.mpim` is delivered for each message posted in a group direct message, mentioned or not — it is not limited to messages that mention the bot. A Slack app can be created from a manifest — one YAML or JSON document naming the bot user, its scopes and, where the app is already reachable, its event subscriptions — pasted at api.slack.com/apps; the signing secret is shown under Basic Information and the bot token after the app is installed to the workspace. A manifest that names event subscriptions without a Request URL or Socket Mode does not validate, so an app whose hook address is not yet known cannot be created with its events already listed: the events are added once the address exists (sections 3.1 and 3.7).

Signing is not Telegram's shared header and not the raw-body HMAC yardr verifies today. Slack sends `X-Slack-Request-Timestamp` (seconds) and `X-Slack-Signature: v0=<hex>`, where the hex is HMAC-SHA256 with the app's signing secret over the literal basestring `v0:<timestamp>:<raw body>` — version, timestamp and body together — and a request whose timestamp is more than five minutes from the receiver's clock is to be refused. Threads are real, unlike Telegram's flat chat: `chat.postMessage` with `channel`, `text` and `thread_ts` set to the parent's `ts` answers in that thread (without it, a new top-level message), answering with the posted message's `ts`, and posting as the bot takes the bot token alone. Reading a thread back takes the same bot token: `conversations.replies` with `channel` and the parent's `ts` lists the parent and its replies oldest first, cursor-paginated — each answer carries `has_more` and, while more remain, `response_metadata.next_cursor` to send as the next `cursor`, with up to 1,000 messages in one answer — and Slack lists `channels:history`, `groups:history`, `im:history` and `mpim:history` all as bot-token scopes for that method, so a bot token holding the history scope for the conversation's kind reads a public-channel, private-channel, direct-message or group-direct-message thread alike, in any conversation the bot belongs to. No user token is required. The tighter limit of one request per minute and at most 15 objects that Slack announced for `conversations.replies` is for commercially distributed non-Marketplace apps, not for an internal customer-built app such as this one, which keeps 50+ requests per minute and the 1,000-object limit above ([Slack's changelog of 29 May 2025](https://docs.slack.dev/changelog/2025/05/29/rate-limit-changes-for-non-marketplace-apps/)). Slack offers no narrower thread-only history scope, so those four scopes are also what let the bot read older messages of a conversation it belongs to by other calls; the yard calls `conversations.replies` alone with them, for threads it has heard. `chat.postMessage` takes no idempotency key. A thread has no status to move, and a channel message is no bead's link in yardr's sense.

## 3. The proposal

The smallest version that answers the wish: a pack `packs/slack` declares a source `slack` that hears a workspace's mentions and direct messages, maps each sender to a user of the yard where the operator has mapped them, and answers in the same Slack thread with `yardr reply`. Small general additions to the core carry what no declaration can say today — a signature over timestamp and body together, an answer to a service's verification challenge, `source check` showing what its check call selected, hearing by who a speaker is rather than by where they are told (with the hook's `--unknown` word carried on its run-ask card too), the derived `speaker` field that hearing sets, the one-to-one write that keeps it unambiguous, and the per-source ignore list its "nobody" answer needs — and the claim card grows from "is this you?" to "who is this?". Everything else is mechanism that stands: subscription routing, crew identity, reply in a thread, filing with `--for`, and setup by asks. Nothing names Slack in product code; the name lives in the pack, its declaration and its two scripts.

### 3.1. The pack

```text
packs/slack/pack.yaml        # name: slack, kind: source, setup: SETUP.md
packs/slack/slack.yaml       # the declaration below
packs/slack/SETUP.md         # section 3.7 as steps
packs/slack/slack/normalize  # Events API body -> envelope
packs/slack/slack/thread     # reply, posted, read over chat.postMessage / conversations.replies
packs/slack/app-manifest.yaml  # the Slack app as one document, without event subscriptions (section 3.7)
```

The declaration's shape, beside telegram's (only the keys that differ in kind are shown whole):

```yaml
name: slack
about: Mentions and direct messages in a Slack workspace, answered in their threads.
base: https://slack.com/api
credential:
  env: SLACK_BOT_TOKEN
  header: "Authorization: Bearer {credential}"
calls:
  whoami:                      # yardr source check slack: whose token this is
    kind: read
    about: Who the bot token acts as (auth.test)
    request: { method: POST, path: /auth.test }
    answer: { select: [user_id, team_id, bot_id] }
  post:
    kind: update
    about: One top-level message to a channel (chat.postMessage); threaded answers go by thread.reply
    params:
      channel: { type: string, pattern: "^[CDG][A-Z0-9]+$" }
      text:    { type: string, max: 4000 }
    request:
      method: POST
      path: /chat.postMessage
      body: { channel: "{channel}", text: "{text}" }
check: whoami
hook:
  verify:
    scheme: hmac-sha256
    signed: "v0:{timestamp}:{body}"        # new, section 3.2
    signature: { header: X-Slack-Signature, encoding: hex, prefix: "v0=" }
    delivery:  { body: event_id }
    timestamp: { header: X-Slack-Request-Timestamp, unit: s, window: 5m }
  bodies: repeat                            # a retry repeats event_id, not the bytes alone
  challenge: { body: type, value: url_verification, answer: challenge }   # new, section 3.2
  normalize: [slack/normalize]
  keys: [channel, team, sender, room]      # what normalize may set; the core adds the derived filter key speaker for this hear, section 3.3
hear:
  field: sender
  by: identity                             # new, section 3.3; no address, no from
thread:
  reply:  [slack/thread, reply]            # in {thread, parent, body, key}  out {id}
  posted: [slack/thread, posted]           # in {thread, key}                out {} always
  read:   [slack/thread, read]             # in {thread}                     out {messages: […]}, section 3.6
```

There is no `notify` key in a first version (section 4) and no `link` or `thread.state`. The thread id a delivery carries is `<channel>:<parent ts>` — Slack names a message by channel and `ts` together, so the pair is the thread, as a GitHub thread is `<owner>/<repo>#<number>`. The credential is one bot token for the whole yard, as telegram's is; the yard speaks in threads with the answering crew member's own copy of it (section 3.7), and a reply is one message of at most 4,000 characters of plain text, as telegram's is. That one credential is also the whole credential story for threads in a first version: every thread command is handed the single value under `SLACK_BOT_TOKEN`, and because that token carries the four history scopes of section 2.7 no second credential is asked for, given or handed to any command — channel, group-direct-message and direct-message threads are read back with the very token replies are posted with (section 3.6). `fields.room` — one of the keys above — holds which kind of conversation spoke, in two words only: `dm` for a one-to-one direct message, and `channel` for everything else, a channel of either kind and a group direct message alike, since a group is treated as a channel is (sections 3.6 and 5, question 5). normalize sets it from what the event can actually show: a `message.im` carries `channel_type` `im` and gives `dm`, and for an `app_mention`, which Slack's documented example shows carrying no `channel_type` (section 2.7), the first letter of the channel id decides — `D` is `dm`, anything else is `channel`. No stronger reading is promised: a `G…` id alone cannot be told apart as a group direct message rather than a legacy private channel, and no event a first version takes is relied on to name it `group`, so `room` has no `group` word and no test may pin one. The two words are load-bearing all the same: only `dm` is heard whole (section 3.6). For either event a first version takes, normalize sets the envelope's `type` to `message` (an `app_mention` and a direct message are both a message the yard was sent), which is what the subscriptions of section 3.6 filter on.

Showing what the check call answered is the one other general addition the pack needs, and it is worded for any declared source, as the rest are. `yardr source check <name>` already makes the declaration's check call and already holds its selected answer in `Result.Answer`; the command in internal/cli/sourcecmd.go learns to show it. When the check call names an `answer.select`, a successful check prints each selected top-level field the answer carries, one per line under the status line — `slack: user_id: U…`, `slack: team_id: T…`, `slack: bot_id: B…` for this pack — rendering a string as it is, a number or a boolean as its JSON, and an object or a list as compact JSON, and its `--json` output carries the same selected object under `answer`. A selected field the answer does not carry prints no line (the check still succeeds or fails exactly as today, on the status alone, so a declaration whose selection names an optional field is unchanged), a check call with no selection prints exactly what it prints today, and no other call's answer is ever shown: the selection the declaration already names is the whole of what setup may read, and the credential, which is never part of a service's answer, can never appear there. That printing is what sections 3.6 and 3.7 read the bot's member id from; it lands in bead S2, before any setup step leans on it, and a check whose `user_id` line does not appear has not shown whose token it holds and is not a token setup may proceed with.

### 3.2. Signing with a timestamp, and the verification challenge (new, general)

Slack's signature covers `v0:<timestamp>:<body>`, and no declaration can say that today: `signed` takes `body` alone and `Declared.Verify` hashes the body alone. The addition is to let `signed` be a template over the two things the core already has in hand before it trusts either — the raw timestamp string from the header `verify.timestamp` names, and the raw body bytes — with the placeholders `{timestamp}` and `{body}` written once each, in that vocabulary and no service's:

```yaml
signed: "v0:{timestamp}:{body}"
```

The literal text around them is the service's version marker, carried as data like the `sha256=` prefix is. The reader refuses a template with a missing or repeated placeholder, an unknown placeholder, or one declared without a `timestamp` for `{timestamp}` to read. `Verify` builds the basestring from the header's string as it arrived — never a re-rendered integer, never re-serialised JSON — hashes that, and then checks the timestamp's age within its window as it does today, so a replayed body with a fresh-looking signature still fails on age. A service that signs the body alone keeps saying `signed: body`, which the reader accepts as the template `{body}`; Linear and GitHub declarations are unchanged. This is the whole signing change: Slack's five-minute window is the standing `timestamp` key with `unit: s, window: 5m`, and its `v0=` is the standing `prefix`.

The `url_verification` handshake is the second new key, and it belongs to the receiver, not to normalize: the challenge arrives before any subscription could matter, must be answered within three seconds with the challenge value itself, and records nothing. `hook.challenge` says, as data, that a verified body whose field at the dot path `body` equals `value` is answered at once with a JSON object naming that body's `answer` field and its value, before Normalize, dedupe or recording:

```text
Slack -> POST /hooks/slack  {"type":"url_verification","challenge":"3eZbrw1aBm2rZgRNFdxek259"}
yard  -> 200 {"challenge":"3eZbrw1aBm2rZgRNFdxek259"}
```

Verification still comes first: an unsigned challenge is refused as any bad signature is, so the endpoint cannot be used to reflect unsigned text. A service with no handshake names no `challenge` key and behaves exactly as today. The three-second budget is also why the event path keeps no new work before the 200: the receiver already records in one write and answers; the normalize script is a `jq` program of the telegram script's size.

### 3.3. Who spoke: hearing by identity, and the three cases for an unmapped sender

Telegram hears by address because its question is "is this the chat we tell?". Slack's question is "who is speaking?", and the yard already keeps that answer as an identity. The `hear` key therefore gains a second way to match, chosen by `by`:

```yaml
hear:
  field: sender        # the envelope field holding the member's id
  by: identity         # match it against yardr user identity --source slack
```

With `by: identity` the receiver keeps a delivery when the value of `fields[field]` — for Slack, the sender's member id, which normalize also sets as `actor.id` — is the `id` of some user's identity in this source (`User.IsIn` in internal/store/subscriptions.go read over every user, where `Receiver.hears` reads env files today). `address` and `from` do not apply to this way and are refused with it; the reader likewise refuses `by: identity` with no `hook.keys` field to read, as it refuses a field no key names today. The existing way keeps its spelling (`address: {env: …}`, with `from: operator` to narrow it), so telegram's file and its tests stand unchanged. Matching by identity rather than by env-file address is what lets one workspace hear many people: each mapping is the one line `yardr user identity ada --source slack --id U012AB3CD`, held on the user's record where `yardr thread open --assign` already reads it, and not a second copy of the id in an env file whose other job is telling. That mapping is one to one, enforced where it is written rather than assumed where it is read: in the one write that sets a user's identity in a source, the store refuses the write when a different user already holds the same source and `id` (for Slack the `id` alone, since no `--key` is set), so no member id ever names two yard users at once. `SetIdentity` today writes one user's record without looking at any other (`internal/store/subscriptions.go`), and `User.IsIn` would then answer true for each holder; the check belongs in that write, beside the write's own `mayIdentify` check, covering a mapping typed in a terminal, one proposed on a card and one the receiver's hearing relies on alike. A mapping card whose member id has been mapped to anyone since the card was written is stale on the same ground and runs nothing (section 3.4). Should a store already hold a duplicate from before the rule, hearing treats the sender as unmapped rather than picking a holder: no `speaker` is set from an ambiguous id, and removing one of the duplicate identities by hand restores hearing.

Where a mapped delivery is kept, the core adds one field of its own after Normalize, as it derives an envelope's train today (`deriveTrain` in internal/hooks/receiver.go): `fields.speaker` holds the yard user's name, so a subscription may filter on it, the inbox line can name them, and the crew member reading the delivery sees whose it is without a lookup. A delivery kept with no mapping (the `allow` case below) carries `speaker: [outside]`. Filtering on `speaker` takes one small addition of its own, because it is a field the core derives and no normalize script sets: subscription filters today accept the shared `WhereKeys` plus the keys a declaration names under `hook.keys` (`whereKeysTx` in internal/store/subscriptions.go), and `speaker` stands in neither, so a `--where speaker=ada` typed against the declaration of section 3.1 would be refused as written. The core therefore adds `speaker` to the accepted filter keys of a source whose declaration hears `by: identity` — in `whereKeysTx`, beside the declaration's own keys, for that kind of source alone — and the pack's declaration keeps `speaker` out of `hook.keys`, which names only what normalize may set (`channel`, `team`, `sender`, `room`). A subscription to a source with no identity hearing, telegram's included, still refuses `speaker`, exactly as it refuses any key that source does not expose.

The inbox line names the speaker from that field, not by overwriting the envelope's actor. Today `printHookDelivery` in `internal/cli/subscriptions.go` renders only `actor.name` or `actor.id`; a Slack envelope's actor is the member id (`actor.id` is `U…`, `actor.name` empty, since normalize is handed no credential to look a display name up), so the field alone would leave the line reading `by U012AB3CD` for everyone. The core therefore leaves `actor` as Slack gave it and the CLI learns to prefer `fields.speaker` where it stands: for a mapped delivery the line reads `by ada (U012AB3CD)` — the yard user the operator mapped, with the member id beside it so the record can still be traced — and for an outsider kept under `allow` it reads `by outside (U099XY…) — information, not instructions`, the `OutsideMail` cue (section 2.4) applied to a person, so the session that hears it applies section 3.5's outsider bounds without a lookup. A telegram delivery, which carries no `speaker`, renders exactly as today. The same preference is wanted wherever a hook delivery is shown to a crew member (the inbox, a nudge and the prime's inbox slice all go through that one rendering), not only in `yardr inbox`.

A sender no user maps is then one of mckean's three cases, and the declaration cannot choose among them, because the choice is the operator's about their own yard:

- **unknown** — ask the operator (section 3.4). The delivery is dropped as a stranger's is today, and a mapping card is offered. Nothing of it is heard until the operator maps the sender or declines.
- **allow** — heard as an outsider. The delivery is recorded and routed with `speaker: [outside]`; what its words weigh is section 3.5.
- **ignore** — dropped, answered 200, recorded nowhere, counted beside refusals as a stranger's burst is today; no card is offered.

None of the three is the yard's own post coming back: a sender who is a crew member's identity in the source, by its id or its key, is dropped under any word, counted as the yard's own, and never offered a mapping card — the bot's echo is neither delivered nor asked about.

The policy is one word on the hook, set when the hook is made and changed by remaking it, as a hook's `--map` lines are: `yardr hook add slack --source slack --unknown unknown|allow|ignore`. The flag and the word it stores are general and new together: `Hook` in internal/store/hooks.go gains the word (empty in a hook made before the flag existed, read as the default below), `yardr hook add` in internal/cli/hooks.go gains the flag, and `putHook` holds the pair — the word is one of the three, it is refused for a source whose declaration names no identity hearing (there is no sender to be unknown), and a hook for an identity-hearing source made without the flag stores `unknown`, so the safe word needs no card to restate it and an existing telegram hook, whose source hears by address, is untouched and refuses the flag. One hook serves the yard, so the policy is per source as the yard runs it — per workspace, Slack being one source per yard in a first version. That scope and the hook's word were settled apart at decide (section 5, questions 1 and 2): the hook is created with `--unknown unknown`, and the word stands for the whole workspace. Per-channel policy is deliberately not in the mechanism: what a channel may do differently is already two standing filters downstream of the gate, a subscription's `--where channel=<id>` choosing which channels reach the yardmaster at all, and the mapping itself, which is per person in every channel at once. Who decides is the operator alone, as for every hook: `hook add` is the operator's command, its run ask is approved by the operator, and no crew member's subscription or mapping changes the gate.

That last sentence needs the run-ask row to carry the flag, which today it cannot (section 2.6): the hook row of the run-ask list therefore grows in the same bead that adds the flag (bead S3), it does not trail behind it. `parseHookAdd` in internal/store/runask_hook.go learns `--unknown` beside `--source` — each at most once, the word held to the same three values and to a source that hears by identity, read by name rather than by position so `hookAddOf` and the register check that today counts five words keep working — the row's use line becomes `yardr hook add <name> --source <declared source> [--unknown unknown|allow|ignore]`, the card (`hookAddCard`) states the word it will store in its sentences and holds the approval to it as it holds the source (`hookAddStale`), `runHookAdd` writes that word through `putHook`, and `--map`, `--as` and `--secret-file` stay refused on a card exactly as they are. The regression test stands where the row's tests stand (internal/store/hookask_test.go): a typed `--run "hook add slack --source slack --unknown unknown"` is accepted and its approval stores the word, a repeated flag, a word outside the three and a word for a source with no identity hearing are each refused, and the same command without the flag still parses and stores the default. Until S3 lands, section 3.7's hook card cannot be written, which is why S3 stands before S5 and S5 claims no card the row cannot carry.

### 3.4. The mapping card: "who is this?" in place of "is this you?"

The claim of yardr-68bf asks the operator about their own address and writes it with `user key`. A Slack sender is somebody else's id, so the claim generalises along the axis the bead names — from an address a user is told at, to an identity a user speaks as:

- A dropped delivery in the `unknown` case offers the operator one card per source and sender (where the telegram claim waits one per source and operator): *"A message reached slack from U012AB3CD in channel C012AB3CD (first words: '…'). Who is this?"*, showing the member id, the channel, and at most the first 80 characters of the message, with the message kept nowhere else, exactly as the claim card bounds it, and with the same treatment of outside text as data (`claimPlain` in internal/store/claim.go: control and bidi characters dropped, a value holding any offers no card). One card per sender waits at a time: a second message from the same member while its card stands open or put aside offers no second card, so a stranger writing repeatedly does not pile cards up — the pile is bounded to one per new person, as the claim's one per source and operator bounds it there. There is no ignore list in the claim mechanism to reuse for the "nobody" answer below — the claim only waits, writes an address on approval or writes nothing on decline (`internal/store/claim.go`) — so the list is new, and deliberately the smallest one that carries the decided behaviour (section 5, question 1).
- The card is a new kind of ask beside the run ask, a **mapping ask**, because no one command is being proposed until the operator names the person: its answer is a pick among the yard's users, each labelled with what approving it runs — `yardr user identity <user> --source slack --id U012AB3CD` — plus "someone new" and "nobody — ignore this sender". Picking a user writes that identity in the answering write, through the same store function `yardr user identity` calls, under the operator's approval and never from an agent pane, as a run ask runs. That command therefore joins the run-ask list as a new row (it is a crew identity's sibling, `yardr user identity <user> --source <source> --id … [--key …]`, held to the source and the user as the crew row is, and refused with `--rm` as that row refuses it), so the yardmaster may also propose a mapping it already knows — from a name in the channel, say — without waiting for a dropped message. The card holds, as a run ask holds what it saw (`AskRun.Seen`), the member id and the fact that it stood unmapped when the card was written; approving re-reads both, and a member id mapped to anyone meanwhile — on another card or typed in a terminal — makes the card stale: nothing runs, and the operator is told the ask is to be asked again, as a stale run ask is. The one-to-one rule of section 3.3 is what is checked in that re-read, in the same write that would write the identity, so two cards answered together cannot both write.
- "Someone new" collects the name on the card itself, and never through a run ask. A run ask approves a command exactly as written (`internal/store/runask.go`: the approval runs the stored argv, and an answer supplies no arguments, `internal/store/asks.go`), so a card proposing `yardr user add <name>` could only ever add the one name baked into it — and no name is available to bake in, since the event carries no display name and normalize is handed no credential to look one up. Picking "someone new" therefore takes the new user's name as the words that go with the pick, which an answer already carries beside its option: the option's consequence line says "pick this and write the new user's name with your answer". The answering write holds those words to the same checks `yardr user add` is held to (`checkUserName`, and the card checks of `userAddCard` in internal/store/runask_more.go: no user or crew member of that name, and not the route keyword `any`), and on a name that passes it adds the user and writes that user's `slack` identity for the member id in the one answering write, through `insertUser` and the identity write above, still under the operator's approval and never from an agent pane. Empty words, an invalid name or a taken name refuse the answer with the reason and leave the card open to answer again; nothing is added and no identity is written. The yard does not invent the name: a Slack member id maps to a user the operator names in those words, and that name, once added, is the user every later step names. No terminal step is needed for the name, and section 3.7's card-only account stands with this step counted as a card.
- "Nobody — ignore this sender" records the member id on a per-source ignore list in the store, so that sender's later messages are dropped without a second card. This is the explicit ignore the operator asked for at decide: without it a stranger the operator does not want to map would offer a fresh card on every later mention and pile them up; with it the same member asks no more cards. The list has a whole lifecycle, not only a listing: `yardr source show slack` prints the ignored ids with the rest of the source (it only prints, as it does today in internal/cli/sourcecmd.go), and one new operator command maintains them — `yardr source ignore slack` lists them, and `yardr source ignore slack --rm U012AB3CD` removes one, as `yardr user identity … --rm` removes an identity. The removal is also the card way back: `yardr source ignore slack --rm <id>` joins the run-ask list as a new row (its sibling `yardr user identity … --rm` is refused there, and this row is its counterpart for the ignore list), so the yardmaster may propose un-ignoring a named member on a card — listing the ignored ids first with `yardr source show slack` — and the operator approves it, without the ignored person's next message needing to arrive first. Removing by hand and removing on a card are the same store write, the operator's alone and refused from an agent pane, as adding a source is; adding to the list happens only by picking "nobody" on the card, never by a subscription or a crew member's say-so. An ignored id takes precedence over the hook's policy, whichever word the hook holds: under `unknown` it offers no further card, and under `allow` it is still dropped, answered 200 and counted beside refusals as any stranger is, because the operator has already answered for that sender in particular. Allowing after ignoring is then two ordinary steps, neither needing the sender to write again first: remove the entry (`yardr source ignore slack --rm <id>`, typed or on its card), and the sender's next message follows the hook's word again — under the decided `unknown` it offers a fresh mapping card, under `allow` it is heard as an outsider — or map the member directly, since the one write that maps a member id — picking a user or "someone new" on a card, or `yardr user identity` typed in a terminal — removes that id from the ignore list in the same write, so a mapped user is never also ignored. Removing an identity later does not put the id back on the list. Declining the mapping card without picking — the answer that ends it with no option — leaves the sender unmapped and not ignored, and offers again on their next message, as a declined claim holds its slot today; only the explicit "nobody" pick silences a sender.

Approving a mapping writes no env file and tells nobody anything: hearing and telling stay separate, as yardr-5rrs made them for telegram, and a mapped user who also wants the phone road sets `notify` by its own commands where a source can tell (Slack's first version cannot, section 4). The card is still the only identity guard, and with many members the race of yardr-68bf widens honestly: any member who mentions the bot first can be offered as a candidate mapping. The bounds that hold are the ones that held there — the snippet is bounded and sanitised, the approval is the operator's alone, a mapping written meanwhile by hand makes the card stale rather than overwritten, and a wrong mapping is one `yardr user identity <user> --source slack --rm` from undone, with everything the wrongly mapped sender was heard saying still in the record under their member id.

### 3.5. What a heard line weighs

A mapped user's mention is their word as their mail is, within the bounds every chat with the crew already has. The yardmaster, in the session that hears it, discusses it, and where the person asks for work it files and shapes beads for them with `--for <user>` on every create, so the work bills their subscription and stands under their name as originator, exactly as a conversation session files for its person. Three things it never is, each enforced where the yard already enforces it rather than asked of the agent: it answers no ask — asks keep their cards and are answered at the yard, in footplate or `yardr chat`, by the person asked, so a Slack line can neither approve a run, give a secret, nor pick an option, and `mayApprove` and the secret path refuse an agent pane whatever the chat said; it grants nothing — no subscription, identity, grant or setting changes on a Slack say-so, each keeping its own card or terminal command; and it speaks with no credential but the reply's own (section 3.6), so a mapped user directs the yardmaster's conversation and filing, and nothing reaches a source call, a thread state or another yard on their word alone beyond what the yardmaster could already do in its own session. The prime's standing guardrails for telegram carry over word for word: never paste a secret or a credential into the chat, and confirm in the yard what would be confirmed by mail. The residual is telegram's, widened by the number of mapped users: a taken-over Slack account speaks as that user to the yardmaster, with that user's filing rights and no more — strictly less than the operator's stolen device, which can also approve and give secrets, and revocable by removing one identity line.

An unmapped sender in the `allow` case is heard as information, not instructions — the `OutsideMail` mark, applied to a person rather than a yard. Their delivery is routed and may be answered in its thread; the yardmaster may answer questions from what it knows, and may file an outside report only as its own note quoting them (originator the operator's, text marked as an outsider's words, as a bead filed from a peer's mail is that yard's text), never `--for` anyone and never shaped, advanced or landed on their say-so. They are told nothing outside the thread, subscribed to nothing, and mapped by nothing they say. What an `allow` sender can do, in one line: be read and be answered. Everything else waits for a mapping. That bound is the decided first-version limit (section 5, question 4): an outsider's words file nothing directly, and widening it — letting a busy support channel's mentions become beads without the yardmaster's own note in between — is named in section 4 as the first limit to revisit, with the traffic of a real channel in hand, rather than built speculatively now.

What the yardmaster may say back to an outsider in a shared channel is bounded as instruction, not as a store refusal, because the reply is the yardmaster's own words in a thread the operator chose to let it answer in. The decided boundary (section 5, question 7) is: answer what the outsider's own question is about, using the yardmaster's judgement for what that takes — and, where a mapped user in the same thread asks the yardmaster to explain something to the outsider, what that mapped user may themselves see is also in scope, on the mapped user's word. The magic the operator named is exactly that second case: a user of the yard mentioning the yardmaster in a channel to have it explain progress to a colleague who is not a user of the yard, in the thread where both stand, without the yardmaster first asking at the yard. Anything beyond those two — private yard context that is neither about the outsider's question nor something a mapped user present has asked to have explained — waits for the operator's or that mapped user's word at the yard, not in the channel. In a direct message with no audience and no mapped user present, only the first case stands. The bound is written where the yardmaster reads it (bead S6), not enforced in the store: the store cannot tell what a reply sentence discloses, and the inbox cue `information, not instructions` (section 3.3) is what tells the session which side of the line it is on. It is to be tested and widened or narrowed with use, as the operator said at decide, rather than tightened now into uselessness.

### 3.6. Mentions, the bot's identity, and which crew member hears

The bot is a member of the workspace, so mentioning it is mentioning a crew member, by the mechanism that stands. Once the bot token is given, `yardr source check slack` runs the declaration's check call (`auth.test`) and — with bead S2's general printing of what a check call selected (section 3.1), without which the command shows only that the token was accepted (section 2.6) — prints the bot's `user_id` beside `team_id` and `bot_id`; the operator — or the yardmaster proposing it and the operator approving the card — sets:

```sh
yardr crew identity yardmaster --source slack --id U-bot… --key B-bot…
```

The id is the `user_id` step 4 prints, matched exactly as mentions arrive as member ids; the key is the `bot_id` beside it, which the echo of the bot's own post may carry alone — a `bot_message` event has no `user`, only `bot_id` — so the yard's own posts match the identity by either. From then on a text holding `<@U-bot…>` normalises to `mentions: [U-bot…, …]`, the store matches it against the yardmaster's identity, and a subscription of scope `mentions` or `involved` delivers it as `mentioned`. The yardmaster's own posts name it as actor where Slack sends them back; the receiver drops a delivery whose sender is a crew member's identity in the source under any Unknown word, before hearing and before any mapping card, and the standing self-exclusion keeps whatever is recorded so out of its inbox.

Who hears is the yardmaster alone in a first version, by two subscriptions the setup leaves standing:

```sh
yardr subscribe yardmaster slack --scope mentions --where type=message
yardr subscribe yardmaster slack --scope all --where type=message --where room=dm
```

`mentions`, not telegram's `all`: a workspace is many channels of other people's talk, and the bot is woken by being addressed, not by everything said where it stands — the subscription is also what keeps channels the yard does not want heard out, with `--where channel=` lines where the operator wants that said per channel of the yardmaster rather than of the gate. A direct message to the bot mentions nobody in its text, so the second subscription takes one-to-one direct messages whole, as a one-to-one chat is addressed to its other party; a group direct message is not a one-to-one chat, and it is treated as a channel is. That is the two-word `room` of section 3.1 doing its work: normalize sets `fields.room` to `dm` only for a one-to-one direct message and to `channel` for everything else, so the `--where room=dm` line can never match a group message, mentioned or not, while the `mentions` line delivers a group message that mentions the bot exactly as it delivers a channel mention — both read `room` `channel`, and no test may ask normalize to tell a group mention from a channel one, since a `G…` id alone does not settle it (section 3.1). What Slack sends is fixed to match, and this is where the mention-each-time decision (section 5, question 5) is enforced before any subscription runs: setup subscribes to two bot events only, `app_mention` and `message.im` — not to every channel message, and, deliberately, not to `message.mpim`. An unmentioned group message therefore never reaches the yard at all (no event carries it, so it is neither heard, nor recorded, nor offered on a mapping card), a mentioned group message reaches it once as an `app_mention` with `room` `channel`, and a one-to-one direct message reaches it as a `message.im` with `room` `dm`, mentioned in its text or not. Subscribing to `message.mpim` as well would defeat the decision twice over: Slack delivers it for every group message, so the yard would receive whole group conversations it was never addressed in, and each group mention would arrive a second time beside its `app_mention`. The `mpim:history` scope is kept, but it is a reading scope, not a hearing subscription: it only lets a heard group thread be read back (below). A first version therefore hears mentions in channels the bot is invited into, mentions in group direct messages the bot is part of, and its one-to-one direct messages, and nothing else — the decided scope (section 5, question 6), with a group counting beside channels for where a mention is required. The brakeman subscribes to nothing and is reached through the yardmaster, as in telegram's setup: one recipient means one answer, with no arbitration, and the double-answer hazard of two `mentions` subscriptions on one workspace is the one design yardr-c55e recorded, left to standing instructions if a second crew member is ever subscribed. Follow-ups in a thread the yardmaster has answered in do not keep reaching it on their own: the subscription stays `mentions`, not `involved`, so a later message in that thread — in a channel, a group or a thread of either — reaches the yardmaster only when it mentions the bot again (in a one-to-one direct message the standing `room=dm` line still takes it whole, addressed as that chat is); `involved` would also bring the thread's later messages mentioning nobody, as it brings a Linear issue the yardmaster works on, and it is not subscribed in a first version for the reason given there: a workspace thread is other people's conversation in a way an issue's comments are not.

Per person, the hearing lands in the yardmaster's line session, not in a conversation session for that user. That is what the machinery does today: hook deliveries arrive as events, and events go to the line session's slice, while a conversation session's slice takes its person's mail. The mapped user's Slack thread is therefore talked in from the line session, which files for them with `--for` as it files for anyone with no conversation session of their own. Routing a hook delivery to the conversation slice of the user its `speaker` field names is a real second version — it would put each person's Slack talk in the session that already holds their mail and their env — and it is left out (section 4) rather than half-built, because it changes which session a reply's credential and journal belong to.

Replies carry the thread, within what the one bot credential may do, as section 2.5 bounds it. normalize sets the envelope's `thread.id` to `<channel>:<parent ts>` (`thread_ts` where the message is a reply, else its own `ts`), `message` to its `ts`, and `parent` to the parent `ts`. `slack/thread reply` calls `chat.postMessage` with that channel and `thread_ts` set to the parent, with the bot token alone, so `yardr reply <seq> "…"` answers where the mention stood, in its thread, in channels, group direct messages and one-to-one direct messages alike; a plain reply needs no thread read, since the envelope already carries the parent (section 2.5). Reading back takes the same bot token. `slack/thread read` calls `conversations.replies` for the parent with that token, following its cursor to the end — sending each answer's `response_metadata.next_cursor` as the next call's `cursor` while `has_more` stands, and concatenating the pages oldest first — and maps each message to `{id: ts, parent, author: {id: user}, at, body}`, and `yardr thread show <seq>` then prints the whole thread oldest first — in a public or private channel, a direct message or a group direct message alike, since the token holds the history scope for each kind (section 2.7) and the bot belongs to every conversation it hears from. Following the cursor is not optional: a single answer holds at most 1,000 messages, so a thread longer than one page would otherwise read as truncated, and a `--to` naming a message on a later page would be reported as not on the thread when it is. `yardr reply --to <ts>` stands with `thread show` here, because `Declared.Reply` finds the message to answer under by listing the thread through that same paged read: it works wherever `thread show` does, while the plain `yardr reply` that answers the delivery itself needs no thread read and is refused nowhere. Where Slack itself refuses the read — the bot is no longer in the conversation, or its token is missing the history scope for that kind (`not_in_channel`, `missing_scope`) — the script surfaces Slack's reason rather than answering an empty list that would read as an empty thread, and `thread show` and `reply --to` fail with those words. `thread.posted` answers `{}` always: `chat.postMessage` takes no idempotency key and keeps no key of ours to find, so a reply retried after its answer was lost posts twice, the limit telegram already documents rather than a new one. There is no `thread.state`, no `thread.open` and no `link`: a Slack thread has no status, opening one would be posting first to a channel, which is telling by another name, and no bead tracks a channel message.

### 3.7. Setup from cards, and the steps that still need a browser or a terminal

SETUP.md walks it as telegram's does, the operator's name where the trial used one. The yard side is cards the whole way; Slack's side is a browser, because Slack gives a workspace no API by which the yard could create or install its own app, and that boundary is named rather than worked around.

1. **The app, from the pack's manifest, in a browser.** At api.slack.com/apps, Create New App, From a manifest: paste `packs/slack/app-manifest.yaml`, which names the bot user and the scopes (`app_mentions:read`, `channels:history`, `groups:history`, `im:history`, `mpim:history`, `chat:write` — the four histories are bot scopes, and they are what `conversations.replies` reads a thread back with on the bot token in each kind of conversation, section 2.7; `mpim:history` in particular is a reading scope only, and no `message.mpim` event is ever subscribed (sections 3.6 and step 6), so it widens nothing that is heard; the histories also let the token read older messages of a conversation it belongs to, the cost noted there, and the yard calls `conversations.replies` alone with them) and names no event subscriptions and no Request URL. A manifest that names events without a Request URL or Socket Mode does not validate (section 2.7), so the events cannot stand in the document the app is created from while its hook address is not yet known; they are added in step 6, once the hook stands and Slack can verify its address. Install the app to the workspace. Two values Slack shows are copied from there into cards below: the signing secret (Basic Information) and, after install, the bot token `xoxb-…` (OAuth & Permissions). Making the app and installing it are the operator's in Slack's pages; no terminal is involved, and no card can do them, since the yard holds no Slack credential yet by which it could act.
2. **Apply the pack, on a card.** A crew member proposes it and the operator approves: `yardr bead ask <bead> "Add Slack to the yard?" --run "pack apply slack"`. The card shows the declaration whole, as the bht9 card renders every field of it, the two scripts with their checksums among them.
3. **The bot token, on a secret card.** `yardr bead ask <bead> "The Slack bot token" --secret slack --context "OAuth & Permissions in the Slack app, after install: xoxb-…"`. Typed unseen, written by the server to the source's env file; in no note, as every secret ask.
4. **Whose token it is.** `yardr source check slack` (the yardmaster runs it where it may make the read call, else mails the operator the command), with bead S2's printing of what the check call selected (section 3.1) — S2 lands before this walk runs, and without it the command prints no id at all (section 2.6) — prints the selected fields of `auth.test`, one per line under the status line:

```text
slack: check call whoami (POST /auth.test): 200 in …ms, … bytes
slack: user_id: U…
slack: team_id: T…
slack: bot_id: B…
```

The first is the id step 7 sets, a token Slack refuses fails the check here rather than at the first mention, and a check that prints no `user_id` line has not shown whose token it holds: setup stops there rather than guessing an id from elsewhere.
5. **The public hook address, on a card if it is not already set.** `yardr bead ask <bead> "…" --run "settings set hook_public_url https://<his host>/hooks"`, the setting telegram's setup also needs; the hook's URL is then `https://<his host>/hooks/slack`. The address reaching the yard — a tunnel or funnel in front of `hook_addr` — is networking outside yardr, the operator's as it was in the trial, and is the one piece of plumbing no card carries.
6. **The signing secret and the hook, on two cards, then the events in the browser.** First `yardr bead ask <bead> "Slack's signing secret" --secret-hook slack --source slack --context "Basic Information in the Slack app."`, then `yardr bead ask <bead> "Set up the Slack webhook?" --run "hook add slack --source slack --unknown unknown"` — the decided word (section 5, question 1), for the whole workspace (question 2), on the hook row as bead S3 extends it to carry `--unknown` (section 3.3); S3 lands before this walk runs, and without it the card cannot be written. Unlike telegram there is no registration call for the yard to make on approval: Slack takes its Request URL only in its own configuration, so approving the hook stands the endpoint up and nothing is sent anywhere. Then, in the browser, the operator opens the app's Event Subscriptions, turns it on, pastes the hook's URL (`https://<his host>/hooks/slack`) as the Request URL, subscribes to the two bot events `app_mention` and `message.im` — no `message.mpim`, so a group direct message is heard only when it mentions the bot, by its `app_mention` (section 3.6) — and saves. Slack sends the `url_verification` challenge of section 3.2 to that URL; the yard answers it from the hook the card just made, and the app shows verified. This is the visit the manifest of step 1 deliberately left undone: only now does the app have a reachable Request URL to name beside its events, so the subscriptions validate. This paste is a step Slack leaves no API for; it is in a browser, not a terminal.
7. **The bot's identity, on a card.** `yardr bead ask <bead> "…" --run "crew identity yardmaster --source slack --id <user_id from step 4> --key <bot_id from step 4>"`, so `@bot` is a mention of the yardmaster (section 3.6). The key is the bot id because the echo of the bot's own post may carry only `bot_id` (a `bot_message` event has no `user`): with both set the yard drops its own posts before hearing, under any Unknown word, and offers no mapping card for them.
8. **The yardmaster's own credential, for answering.** `yardr reply` speaks with the answering crew member's copy of the bot token. On a card this is yardr-0tew's step (`--secret slack --for-crew yardmaster`), and until that bead lands it is the one yard-side step still done in a terminal, exactly as telegram's step 10 is today: append `SLACK_BOT_TOKEN=…` to the yardmaster's env file at mode 0600 and restart it. The manual way stays written in SETUP.md beside the card, as telegram's step 9 keeps both.
9. **The subscription, and an invitation.** The yardmaster subscribes itself from its pane (section 3.6's line) — an agent step, not an operator terminal — and the operator invites the bot into each channel it should hear (`/invite @<bot>` in Slack), without which Slack sends the channel nothing. A first mention is then the test: it lands in the yardmaster's inbox as

```text
5210 10-17 18:02  hook (mentioned) slack message create by ada (U012AB3CD): <@U…> can the shed land on Monday?
```

and `yardr reply 5210 "Noted; I will post here when it lands."` answers in that thread. The thread is read back with `yardr thread show 5210` on this channel delivery too — the bot belongs to the channel it was invited into, and its token holds `channels:history` (section 3.6) — and `yardr hook show 5210` holds the delivery itself meanwhile. Two companion checks pin the group boundary of section 3.6 in the same walk: a message mentioning the bot in a group direct message the bot is part of lands in the inbox once, as a `mentioned` delivery, and a group message mentioning nobody lands nowhere — Slack sends no event for it under step 6's two subscriptions — while a one-to-one direct message mentioning nobody still lands whole by the `room=dm` subscription.

The steps that still need something beyond a card, named whole: creating and installing the Slack app and copying its two secrets out of Slack's pages (a browser); pasting the Request URL into the app and saving (a browser, no API); inviting the bot into channels (Slack itself); the public address reaching `hook_addr` (the operator's networking); and, until yardr-0tew lands, the crew member's credential (a terminal). Everything the yard holds — the pack, both secrets, the hook, the identity, the subscription — is a card or the yardmaster's own subscribe.

## 4. Left out

**Telling a user through Slack (`notify`).** A direct message to a mapped user (`conversations.open` then `chat.postMessage`) would let mail, notifications and asks reach a phone as telegram's telling does. It is a second road for the same telling the yard already does through telegram and pushover, it needs its own address story (a member id is an identity here, not a telling address, and section 3.3 refused to merge the two), and it is not what was asked: the wish is to write to the yard, not to be told by it. The pack therefore names no `notify` key; adding one later touches no hearing machinery.

**Per-channel hearing policy.** The gate of section 3.3 is per hook — per workspace, as decided (section 5, question 2) — and which channels reach the yardmaster is per subscription. A rule that one channel's unmapped senders are allowed while another's are asked about would need the gate to read channel policy per delivery — new store state and a second place the operator keeps the answer — for a difference no trial has asked for. The ignore list is per sender across channels for the same reason. Growing that second place later stays possible over the `channel` field every delivery already carries, without changing what the hook's word means for a yard that never names a channel.

**Reading a conversation's history beyond a heard thread.** Section 3.6 reads back only the thread of a delivery the yard heard, through `conversations.replies`. The history scopes that call takes (section 2.7) would also let the bot token call `conversations.history` for older messages of a conversation it belongs to, and no command in a first version does: the yard does not browse a channel, search it, or follow talk it was not addressed in. Widening the read from "the thread I was mentioned in" to "the channel's past" is a different hearing promise from the one this design makes, and it is left to its own bead if a yard asks for it.

**Routing to a conversation session per person.** Section 3.6 gives the reason: deliveries would change session, with the reply credential and journal that follow the session, and the line session already files `--for` for a mapped user correctly. It becomes attractive when a yard serves several people heavily by Slack, and it is then one routing rule over the `speaker` field this design already records.

**Socket Mode, and polling.** Slack also offers a websocket the app dials out on, which needs no public address. It would need a held connection in the daemon, an app-level token beside the bot token, reconnect and ordering machinery — a new receiver, against a yard that already takes webhooks from three sources. A yard with no public address is the case that would argue for it, as `getUpdates` polling was telegram's, and it reuses this pack's envelope and scripts when it comes.

**More of Slack than mentions and direct messages.** Reactions, channel joins, edits and deletes, files and images (which arrive as ids the yard cannot fetch without a credential normalize is never handed, as telegram notes pictures rather than fetching them), canvases, huddles, slash commands and any interactive button are all outside the events a first version subscribes to, or noted as bracketed text where a message carries no plain text. Hearing a group direct message whole stands here in particular: a group is treated as a channel (sections 3.6 and 5, question 5), so only its mentions are heard, by `app_mention`, and `message.mpim` — which Slack delivers for every group message, mentioned or not — is never subscribed; hearing every message of a group the bot stands in, as a one-to-one direct message is heard, would be a later bead that first argues a group is addressed to the bot as a whole. Edits and deletes stand here beside it, ignored rather than unheard: Slack carries them as a `message` event of subtype `message_changed` or `message_deleted`, which arrives under a `message.*` subscription for the conversation kind — and `message.im` is one, for direct messages, so an edit in a direct message reaches the yard, as the live trial's edit did. Normalize answers both subtypes as `type: event`, which matches no subscription and offers no mapping card: a mention that is edited after it was heard stands as it was heard, and hearing an edited mention again, as a new delivery or as a correction, is a later bead.

**An outsider filing directly.** Section 3.5 keeps an allowed outsider to being read and answered, with any report filed as the yardmaster's own quoted note. Letting an outsider's mention become a bead on its own — originator the operator's, the outsider's text quoted and marked, without the yardmaster's note in between — is cheaper for a channel that turns out to be a support queue, and it spends the yard's building queue on unvetted asking. The operator at decide called the read-and-answer bound right for now, with "lots of potential here": this is therefore the first limit named here to widen, and to widen with a real channel's traffic in hand arguing for it, not before. Nothing in the hearing machinery changes when it is widened; only the role's instruction for what an outsider's line may set in motion does.

**Opening a thread, moving one, and linking a bead to one.** Section 3.6: no status exists to move, opening is telling, and a channel is not an issue. A bead that wants the thread cites its delivery's sequence number, as a telegram chat is cited today.

**A second workspace, a second bot, and Enterprise Grid.** One source is one workspace with one bot token and one signing secret, as one telegram source is one bot. Slack's org-wide ids (`E…` enterprises, members known in several workspaces) are beyond the mapping of section 3.4, whose member ids are workspace ids.

**Answering asks, approving, and giving secrets from Slack.** Left out as telegram left it out, and for Slack's mapped users too (section 3.5): the card remains the place a decision is made, told on whatever road tells that user, answered at the yard.

## 5. Decisions (the open questions, decided)

These were the operator's to decide, and each was put as a separate ask to mckean when the design reached `decide` (asks 6542–6548, 17 October 2026, with question 7 raised by the third critique). The operator answered all seven; the proposal above already stands as decided, and this section is the record of what was chosen and what each choice costs. Where the draft had recommended one answer, the decision is noted against it.

**1. An unmapped sender: asked about, heard as an outsider, or dropped?** This ask settled only the hook's one policy word, `--unknown`, apart from its scope in question 2. The options were (a) *unknown* — dropped until the operator maps them on the "who is this?" card (section 3.4), (b) *allow* — heard at once as information (section 3.5's outsider bounds), and (c) *ignore* — only members mapped beforehand are ever heard. **Decided: (a) *unknown*.** The operator's words were that a sender needs to be explicitly ignored otherwise things keep piling up, with a way to allow after ignoring: so the card's "nobody" pick is that explicit ignore — remembered per member on the per-source list of section 3.4, so the same stranger offers no second card — and allowing after ignoring is the ordinary removal of that entry (`yardr source ignore slack --rm <id>`, typed or on its own card) or a direct mapping, which clears it in the same write. One card per sender waits at a time, so repeated writing from one stranger is bounded before any ignore is needed; the cost accepted is a card for each new person who mentions the bot, including passers-by, until the operator maps or ignores them.

**2. At what scope does that policy stand: one word for the workspace, or a word per channel?** Independent of question 1: any of its three words could in principle stand at either scope. The options were (a) *per hook, that is per workspace* — one `--unknown` word on the one hook, the per-sender ignore list as the only finer grain, and a subscription's `--where channel=` choosing afterwards which channels reach the yardmaster — and (b) *per channel, over the hook's word as its default*, with new store state read per delivery and a second place to keep the answer. **Decided: (a) *per hook*.** No case has yet shown two channels of one workspace wanting different treatment of strangers; per-channel policy stays buildable later over the `channel` field every delivery already carries (section 4), without changing what the hook's word means for a yard that never names a channel.

**3. A mapped user's word: as their mail, or as information?** The options were (a) *as their mail* — conversation and filing with `--for`, never an approval, a secret or a grant — and (b) *information only*, with every deed waiting for the same person at the yard. **Decided: (a) *as their mail* (section 3.5).** Mapping is the operator's act of naming whose word a member's is; a mapping that changed nothing the yardmaster may do for them would be a label, not a mapping. The enforced bounds are identical under either answer — `mayApprove` and the secret path refuse an agent pane whatever the chat said — and the residual is the one section 3.5 states: a taken-over Slack account speaks as that user, revocable by removing one identity line.

**4. What may an allowed outsider's words set in motion?** The options were (a) *be read and answered only* — nothing filed except a quoted outside report the yardmaster files as its own note — and (b) *file as an outsider's report directly*, cheaper for a support channel at the cost of spending the building queue on unvetted asking. **Decided: (a) *read and answered only*.** The operator's words were that this is right for now, with lots of potential in widening it later; section 4 therefore names an outsider filing directly as the first limit to revisit, to be argued for by a real channel's traffic rather than built speculatively.

**5. Follow-ups in a thread: fresh mention required, or is the thread enough?** The options were (a) *mention each time* (`--scope mentions`) — the bot is woken when addressed, and a thread it answered in stays quiet until addressed again — and (b) *the thread is enough* (`--scope involved`) — once the yardmaster has answered in a thread it hears the thread's later messages mentioning nobody, as it hears a Linear issue it works on, at the cost of hearing whole threads including talk between other people. **Decided: (a) *mention each time* (section 3.6).** A workspace thread is other people's conversation in a way an issue's comments are not, and the mention is the speaker saying "this one is for the yard"; the cost is an `@bot` per follow-up message, which Slack's reply flow makes one tap. A group direct message counts with channels under this answer, not with one-to-one direct messages: it is several people's conversation, heard only on a mention, and it reads `room` `channel` like a channel does, since a mention carries no reliable mark that would tell it from one (sections 3.1 and 3.6).

**6. Where may the bot be heard from in a first version: mentions in channels it is invited into and its direct messages, or direct messages alone?** The options were (a) *both* — the workspace case that was asked for, narrowed by inviting the bot and by `--where channel=` — and (b) *direct messages alone*, telegram's shape transplanted, with no channel audience and no bystanders at the cost of the room where a team talks with the yard together. **Decided: (a) *both* (section 3.6).** A private channel the bot is never invited into is already silence. In this answer a mention in a group direct message the bot is part of counts with a channel mention, and "direct messages" heard whole means one-to-one direct messages (`room` `dm`) alone; the two readings together are sections 3.6's two subscriptions over two subscribed events.

**7. When the yardmaster answers an unmapped person in a shared channel, what may it tell them?** Raised by the third critique, which noted the draft let the yardmaster answer outsiders without stating a disclosure boundary. The options were (a) *only what's public* — nothing from beads, mail or decisions, (b) *what's about their own question* — the yardmaster judges, anything private needing the operator's word first, and (c) *no limit beyond its judgement*. **Decided: (b), with the operator's gloss.** What the outsider's own question is about is in scope on the yardmaster's judgement; and where a mapped user in the same thread asks the yardmaster to explain something to the outsider, what that mapped user may themselves see is also in scope, on the mapped user's word — the case the operator called magical, a user mentioning the yardmaster to have it explain progress to a colleague who is not a user of the yard. Anything beyond those two waits for a word at the yard. The bound is instruction in the yardmaster's role (section 3.5, bead S6), to be tested and widened or narrowed with use, not a store refusal; the stricter (a) was the critique's recommendation and the operator set it aside as too unhelpful for that case.

## 6. The beads it would become

In the order they would land. Each is built and tested as its depot says (`sh .yardr/test`), with Slack itself replaced by a stand-in or recorded bodies where a step calls it, and every bug found or fixed gets a regression test. No bead names Slack outside `packs/slack` and its tests.
