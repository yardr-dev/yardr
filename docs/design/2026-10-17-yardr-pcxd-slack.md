# Slack, a bot people mention, heard from many users, each mapped to a user of the yard or not

Design yardr-pcxd, proposed 2026-10-17. A proposal as it was decided on, not a description of what the code does today; the decision is on the bead.

A yard is one installation of yardr: its store, its crew and its depots. The crew are the yard's always-on agents, tied to no bead; the yardmaster is the crew member that coordinates. A user is a person of the yard: a name and an inbox. The operator is the person who runs the yard. A source is an outside system the yard hears from, declared by one YAML file and no Go; a pack is a way of working as one directory, and a pack of kind `source` is one such integration, imported into the yard itself. A hook is a webhook endpoint that turns an outside system's deliveries into events. An envelope is one delivery in the source-neutral shape routing reads. A thread is a conversation in a source that a crew member takes part in. An ask is one question as data on a bead; a run ask proposes one yardr command of a fixed list and the operator's approval runs exactly it; a secret ask asks for a credential typed unseen and written to a file by the server. See docs/glossary.md for all of yardr's words. In this document **Slack** is the chat service, a **workspace** is one Slack organisation, a **member** is a person in it (named there by an id such as `U012AB3CD`), a **channel** is one room of it (`C012AB3CD`), and **the bot** is the yard's Slack app acting as one member of the workspace.

## 1. The question

mckean, 17 Oct 2026 (mail, during the Telegram trial yardr-b6sq): "we should be able to setup a bot that can be mentioned @... I guess you could be listening and responding, the trickier part is having multiple users being able to mention, perhaps we have different scenarios for unknown, allow or ignore, and then being able to map other users to the users in the yard." So: a bot in a Slack workspace that people mention, heard from many people at once — the opposite of Telegram's one bot hearing one chat — where each speaker may already be mapped to a user of the yard or may not, with a stated answer for each of the unmapped cases, set up from cards alone as far as the Telegram trial reached. No code in this bead.

## 2. What exists today

### 2.1. Packs of kind source: telegram, linear, github

`packs/telegram/` (pack.yaml, telegram.yaml, SETUP.md, and the two command files under `packs/telegram/telegram/`) is the nearest pack: kind `source`, name `telegram`, declaring one source with a `notify` key (one message to one chat through the Bot API's `sendMessage`), a `hook` key that hears the operator's chat back, `thread` keys that answer in it, and a `hear` key. `packs/linear/` and `packs/github/` are the same shape for an issue tracker and a forge: a declaration (linear.yaml, github.yaml), a normalize command and a thread command as shell scripts around `jq` and `curl`, held by `internal/hooks` to golden files. Package packs embeds them; `yardr pack apply <name>` imports one into the yard and into no depot. The rule this design follows is the bead's: no service is named in product code, so Slack is a pack, `packs/slack`, as telegram and linear are, and every general mechanism below is worded for any declared source.

### 2.2. Receiving: verify, normalize, dedupe, route

The yard's server takes webhooks as `POST /hooks/<name>` on the `hook_addr` setting (docs/integrations.md, "How one becomes part of a yard"). `internal/hooks/receiver.go` (`Receiver.serve`) reads the hook's secret file on every delivery, runs the adapter's `Verify`, then `Normalize`, records the delivery with its dedupe keys in one store write, and answers 200; a duplicate is answered 200 and dropped with no event.

For a declared source, verification is data in the declaration's `hook.verify` (`internal/source/hook.go`: `HookVerify`, `HookSignature`, `HookDelivery`, `HookTimestamp`). Exactly two schemes stand today. `hmac-sha256` (`HookScheme`) is the hex HMAC-SHA256 of the raw body alone with the hook's secret, in the header the file names, past an optional prefix, with an optional timestamp in the body or a header checked after the signature within a window (`Declared.Verify` in internal/hooks/declared.go does `mac.Write(body)` and nothing else). `secret` (`HookSecret`) is the header holding the hook's secret itself, compared as it arrives in constant time, as Telegram sends it. Linear uses the first with a body timestamp; GitHub uses the first with a `sha256=` prefix and no timestamp; Telegram uses the second. `hookSchemes` in internal/source/hook.go refuses any other scheme, `signed` covers `body` alone, and the encoding is hex alone.

Normalize for a declared source runs `hook.normalize` with the verified body and no credential (its environment is `PATH`, `HOME` and `YARDR_SOURCE_BASE` only), on stdin `{"headers": {…}, "body": …}`, and the core sets `source` and `delivery` itself and refuses an envelope without a `type` (internal/hooks/hooks.go: `Envelope` — `type`, `action`, `actor` as id and name, `thread` as id, title and URL, `mentions` as ids or profile keys, `message` and `parent`, `text` truncated to 500 runes, and `fields`, the keys `hook.keys` names, which a subscription's `--where` may then filter on). Dedupe keys (`DedupeKeys`) are the body's hash under the source or the hook as `hook.bodies` says, plus the delivery id under the hook when the service sent one.

Routing is by subscription (`yardr subscribe <name> <source> --scope … --where …`): `all` is every delivery, `mentions` the deliveries that mention the crew member, `involved` those plus what is assigned or delegated to it and the threads it takes part in (docs/subscriptions.md). A delivery whose `actor.id` is the crew member's own identity in the source never reaches it, which keeps the echo of its own reply out of its inbox. A card may also register a hook with its service (`hook.register` in internal/source/hook.go), but only with scheme `secret`: the yard makes the secret and calls the register call on approval (`RegistersSecret`); with a signature the service makes, registering stays a typed secret in a terminal.

### 2.3. Hearing by address, and the operator's word (telegram, yardr-5rrs, yardr-c55e)

Telegram hears one chat. Its declaration says (`packs/telegram/telegram.yaml`):

```yaml
hear:
  field: chat
  address: { env: TELEGRAM_CHAT_ID }
  from: operator
```

After Normalize and before the delivery is recorded, the core reads `fields[field]` and drops the delivery — answered 200, recorded nowhere, counted beside refusals — unless one of its values equals the address in a user's env file under `address.env`, read as telling reads it (`Receiver.hears` in internal/hooks/receiver.go; `Hear` in internal/source/notify.go). Since yardr-5rrs (merged) the address alone is the consent to be heard, whatever that user is told through: the operator may be told through pushover and still chat through telegram. `from: operator` narrows hearing to the yard's operator (the `operator` setting), so another user who sets the same address is dropped like a stranger. The declaration reader refuses a `hear` with no `hook` and no `notify` key, a field that is no key of `hook.keys`, and an address variable that is not the one `notify` reads: hearing and telling share one address variable by construction.

Design yardr-c55e settled what a heard line weighs, and SETUP.md states it: the yardmaster treats a line from the operator's chat as the operator's word, as their mail — it discusses, shapes, builds and lands what they ask there — with two bounds enforced in the store rather than in instructions. Approving a run ask is the operator's alone and is refused from any agent pane whatever actor it names (`mayApprove` in internal/store/runask.go), and giving a secret answers only to the operator (`internal/store/secretask.go`); a number typed in the chat answers no ask, because nothing matches chat text to asks. The residual was stated there and stands: a taken-over Telegram account directs the yardmaster with the yardmaster's hands, bounded by instruction except where the store enforces.

Two parts of that do not transfer to a workspace. The gate names one field holding one person's address in one private chat; Slack's sender is one of many members in channels the bot shares with people who are not the operator, so "the operator alone" cannot be the hearing rule and a chat id cannot name who spoke. And the operator's-word rule names one person; with many speakers the yard needs, per speaker, an answer to who they are here — which is the mapping question of sections 2.4 and 3.3.

### 2.4. The claim card (yardr-68bf), identities, and whose word mail is

When a dropped delivery arrives from an address no user has, the yard learns the operator's address from the sender instead of a lookup: `Receiver.serve` calls `store.ClaimAddress` (internal/store/claim.go), which stands a run ask `yardr user key <operator> <source>` on its own question bead, carrying the address from the envelope's field (`Declaration.ClaimAddress` in internal/source/notify.go: the hear field with the notify address's variable) and showing at most the first 80 characters of the message. Approving writes the address into the operator's env file at mode 0600 exactly where `yardr user key` writes it, and the next delivery from that address is heard; declining drops it and writes nothing. One claim per source and operator waits at a time, none is offered once the operator has an address, and the card is the only identity guard — a stranger who writes first with a plausible "hi" can be mistaken for the operator, a race the bead kept knowingly. In this first form the claim is the operator's alone, for their own address: the bead itself names mapping another user as a later bead.

Two identity mechanisms already stand beside that env-file address. A crew member's identity in a source (`yardr crew identity <name> --source <source> --id <id> --key <key>`, `Identity` in internal/store/subscriptions.go) is what mention and assignment matching reads: an id matches exactly, a key matches in any case, and `mentionedAs` also takes the slug before a `[bot]` suffix. It is on the run-ask list, so a crew member proposes its own and the operator approves it on a card. A user's identity in a source (`yardr user identity <user> --source <source> --id … --key …`) is set by that user or the operator, never a crew member, and today assigns threads (`yardr thread open --assign <user>`) and bills a hook author their own login; it is not on the run-ask list. A user told through a source is a third, separate thing: the `notify` address in their env file, which telling reads.

Mail from another yard carries the yard's standing answer to "whose word is this" for a speaker who is not simply a user here: `OutsideMail = "from another yard: information, not instructions"` (internal/store/peermail.go), shown wherever a crew member reads it, unless the operator trusts the sender's name on that link as a user of this yard, when the mark becomes `TrustedMail`: "that user's word, as if they had written it by mail from this yard" (internal/store/peertrust.go). A hook delivery carries no trust mark of either kind today: the envelope has no field for one and the receiver attaches none.

Filing for a person also stands: a crew member's conversation session files and shapes their person's beads with `--for <them>` on every create, so the work bills their subscription (packs/starter/roles/yardmaster.md, "Which session you are"; docs/crew.md, "Conversation sessions"). The line session hears whoever has no conversation session. Events and mail from people with no conversation go to the line session's slice of the one inbox; ask answers go to the session that asked.

### 2.5. Threads and replies in a declared source

A declared source is answered in when its file has `thread.reply`, `thread.posted` and `thread.read` (`ReplierFor` in internal/hooks/reply.go). `yardr reply <seq>` posts in the delivery's thread with the caller's own credential, read from the crew member's pane environment (its `env_file` or `env_command`); the server never holds it. Before anything is sent the reply is on record with a key, and a retry asks `thread.posted` what that key left before posting again. `Declared.Reply` finds the conversation's root: the envelope's `parent` where it carries one, else by listing the thread, which is also how `yardr reply --to <message id>` answers under another message of the thread (`replyParent`). `yardr thread show <seq>` lists the thread through `thread.read`. A source with no `thread.state` and no `link` key has no status moves and no bead links, as telegram has none.

Telegram is the limiting case: `thread.posted` answers `{}` always and `thread.read` answers `{messages: []}` always, because the Bot API gives a bot no history and takes no idempotency key, so a retried reply whose answer was lost posts twice and `reply --to` is refused. Linear is the full case: `thread.read` pages `conversations`-style queries, `thread.posted` finds the comment by the key the service accepted as its own id, and `thread.state` moves an issue among the team's statuses.

### 2.6. Setup from cards alone, and what the Telegram trial hit (yardr-b6sq, yardr-0tew)

The trial's rule is that every step reaches the operator as a card they answer, and every step that still needed a terminal or a paste outside a card became a bead. What stands on cards today: applying an embedded pack (a run ask, design yardr-bht9); a source's credential (`yardr bead ask … --secret <source>`, typed unseen, written by the server); a user's own address in a source (`--secret <source> --for-user <user>`); a hook's signing secret (`--secret-hook <hook> --source <source>`, into `<home>/hooks/<hook>.secret` at 0600) followed by the hook itself as a run ask (`--run "hook add <name> --source <source>"`, internal/store/runask_hook.go), or, with scheme `secret`, one hook card on which the yard makes the secret and registers the webhook itself; the public hook address as a run ask (`settings set hook_public_url …`, which binds nothing); a crew member's identity as a run ask (`--run "crew identity …"`). The one step the trial found with no card is the crew member's own credential for answering in a source: `yardr reply` speaks with the answering crew member's env file, and giving it is still `umask`, `printf >>`, `chmod`, `crew set env_file`, `crew restart` by hand (packs/telegram/SETUP.md step 10). That is bead yardr-0tew (open, stage `new`): `--secret <source> --for-crew <crew>` beside `--for-user`, for every declared source with a reply, Slack's included when it lands. The trial also left the networking outside yardr: a public address reaching `hook_addr` (there, a Tailscale Funnel path) is the operator's, as is any proxy in front of `yardr web`.

### 2.7. Slack's API, as read

Read at api.slack.com on 17 October 2026 (methods `auth.test`, `chat.postMessage`, `conversations.replies`; Events API; signing requests; app manifests). Slack is an HTTPS JSON API at `https://slack.com/api/<method>` with the bot's token (`xoxb-…`) as `Authorization: Bearer <token>`. `auth.test` answers the bot's own ids: `user_id` (the bot member's `U…` id), `team_id` and `bot_id`.

Receiving is the Events API. The app's Request URL is set in the app's configuration (by hand, or from an app manifest the app was created with); Slack first POSTs `{"type": "url_verification", "challenge": "…"}` there and expects the challenge echoed back as JSON within three seconds, and expects every event answered within three seconds, retrying with `X-Slack-Retry-Num` when it is not. Each event is a POST of `{"type": "event_callback", "team_id": "T…", "event_id": "Ev…", "event": {…}}`; a message event carries `type: "message"` (or the dedicated `app_mention` event when the bot is mentioned), `user` (the sender's `U…` id), `channel` (`C…`, or a direct-message id), `text`, `ts` (the message's own id, unique per channel) and, in a thread, `thread_ts` (the `ts` of the thread's first message; every reply carries it, and the first message is its own parent). Mentions stand in the text as `<@U…>`; channel and link references have their own bracketed forms. The bot only sees channels it has been invited into, plus its direct messages, under the scopes the manifest asks for (`app_mentions:read`, `channels:history`, `groups:history`, `im:history`, `chat:write` among them). A Slack app can be created from a manifest — one YAML or JSON document naming the bot user, its scopes, its event subscriptions and its Request URL placeholder — pasted at api.slack.com/apps; the signing secret is shown under Basic Information and the bot token after the app is installed to the workspace.

Signing is not Telegram's shared header and not the raw-body HMAC yardr verifies today. Slack sends `X-Slack-Request-Timestamp` (seconds) and `X-Slack-Signature: v0=<hex>`, where the hex is HMAC-SHA256 with the app's signing secret over the literal basestring `v0:<timestamp>:<raw body>` — version, timestamp and body together — and a request whose timestamp is more than five minutes from the receiver's clock is to be refused. Threads are real, unlike Telegram's flat chat: `chat.postMessage` with `channel`, `text` and `thread_ts` set to the parent's `ts` answers in that thread (without it, a new top-level message), answering with the posted message's `ts`; `conversations.replies` with `channel` and the parent's `ts` lists the parent and its replies oldest first. `chat.postMessage` takes no idempotency key. A thread has no status to move, and a channel message is no bead's link in yardr's sense.

## 3. The proposal

The smallest version that answers the wish: a pack `packs/slack` declares a source `slack` that hears a workspace's mentions and direct messages, maps each sender to a user of the yard where the operator has mapped them, and answers in the same Slack thread with `yardr reply`. Three small general additions to the core carry what no declaration can say today — a signature over timestamp and body together, an answer to a service's verification challenge, and hearing by who a speaker is rather than by where they are told — and the claim card grows from "is this you?" to "who is this?". Everything else is mechanism that stands: subscription routing, crew identity, reply in a thread, filing with `--for`, and setup by asks. Nothing names Slack in product code; the name lives in the pack, its declaration and its two scripts.

### 3.1. The pack

```text
packs/slack/pack.yaml        # name: slack, kind: source, setup: SETUP.md
packs/slack/slack.yaml       # the declaration below
packs/slack/SETUP.md         # section 3.7 as steps
packs/slack/slack/normalize  # Events API body -> envelope
packs/slack/slack/thread     # reply, posted, read over chat.postMessage / conversations.replies
packs/slack/app-manifest.yaml  # the Slack app as one document (section 3.7)
```

The declaration's shape, beside telegram's (only the keys that differ in kind are shown whole):

```yaml
name: slack
about: Mentions and direct messages in a Slack workspace, answered in their threads.
base: https://slack.com/api
credential:
  env: SLACK_BOT_TOKEN
  header: "Authorization: Bearer {credential}"
calls:
  whoami:                      # yardr source check slack: whose token this is
    kind: read
    about: Who the bot token acts as (auth.test)
    request: { method: POST, path: /auth.test }
    answer: { select: [user_id, team_id, bot_id] }
  post:
    kind: update
    about: One top-level message to a channel (chat.postMessage); threaded answers go by thread.reply
    params:
      channel: { type: string, pattern: "^[CDG][A-Z0-9]+$" }
      text:    { type: string, max: 4000 }
    request:
      method: POST
      path: /chat.postMessage
      body: { channel: "{channel}", text: "{text}" }
check: whoami
hook:
  verify:
    scheme: hmac-sha256
    signed: "v0:{timestamp}:{body}"        # new, section 3.2
    signature: { header: X-Slack-Signature, encoding: hex, prefix: "v0=" }
    delivery:  { body: event_id }
    timestamp: { header: X-Slack-Request-Timestamp, unit: s, window: 5m }
  bodies: repeat                            # a retry repeats event_id, not the bytes alone
  challenge: { body: type, value: url_verification, answer: challenge }   # new, section 3.2
  normalize: [slack/normalize]
  keys: [channel, team, sender, room]
hear:
  field: sender
  by: identity                             # new, section 3.3; no address, no from
thread:
  reply:  [slack/thread, reply]            # in {thread, parent, body, key}  out {id}
  posted: [slack/thread, posted]           # in {thread, key}                out {} always
  read:   [slack/thread, read]             # in {thread}                     out {messages: […]}
```

There is no `notify` key in a first version (section 4) and no `link` or `thread.state`. The thread id a delivery carries is `<channel>:<parent ts>` — Slack names a message by channel and `ts` together, so the pair is the thread, as a GitHub thread is `<owner>/<repo>#<number>`. The credential is one bot token for the whole yard, as telegram's is; the yard speaks in threads with the answering crew member's own copy of it (section 3.7), and a reply is one message of at most 4,000 characters of plain text, as telegram's is.

### 3.2. Signing with a timestamp, and the verification challenge (new, general)

Slack's signature covers `v0:<timestamp>:<body>`, and no declaration can say that today: `signed` takes `body` alone and `Declared.Verify` hashes the body alone. The addition is to let `signed` be a template over the two things the core already has in hand before it trusts either — the raw timestamp string from the header `verify.timestamp` names, and the raw body bytes — with the placeholders `{timestamp}` and `{body}` written once each, in that vocabulary and no service's:

```yaml
signed: "v0:{timestamp}:{body}"
```

The literal text around them is the service's version marker, carried as data like the `sha256=` prefix is. The reader refuses a template with a missing or repeated placeholder, an unknown placeholder, or one declared without a `timestamp` for `{timestamp}` to read. `Verify` builds the basestring from the header's string as it arrived — never a re-rendered integer, never re-serialised JSON — hashes that, and then checks the timestamp's age within its window as it does today, so a replayed body with a fresh-looking signature still fails on age. A service that signs the body alone keeps saying `signed: body`, which the reader accepts as the template `{body}`; Linear and GitHub declarations are unchanged. This is the whole signing change: Slack's five-minute window is the standing `timestamp` key with `unit: s, window: 5m`, and its `v0=` is the standing `prefix`.

The `url_verification` handshake is the second new key, and it belongs to the receiver, not to normalize: the challenge arrives before any subscription could matter, must be answered within three seconds with the challenge value itself, and records nothing. `hook.challenge` says, as data, that a verified body whose field at the dot path `body` equals `value` is answered at once with a JSON object naming that body's `answer` field and its value, before Normalize, dedupe or recording:

```text
Slack -> POST /hooks/slack  {"type":"url_verification","challenge":"3eZbrw1aBm2rZgRNFdxek259"}
yard  -> 200 {"challenge":"3eZbrw1aBm2rZgRNFdxek259"}
```

Verification still comes first: an unsigned challenge is refused as any bad signature is, so the endpoint cannot be used to reflect unsigned text. A service with no handshake names no `challenge` key and behaves exactly as today. The three-second budget is also why the event path keeps no new work before the 200: the receiver already records in one write and answers; the normalize script is a `jq` program of the telegram script's size.

### 3.3. Who spoke: hearing by identity, and the three cases for an unmapped sender

Telegram hears by address because its question is "is this the chat we tell?". Slack's question is "who is speaking?", and the yard already keeps that answer as an identity. The `hear` key therefore gains a second way to match, chosen by `by`:

```yaml
hear:
  field: sender        # the envelope field holding the member's id
  by: identity         # match it against yardr user identity --source slack
```

With `by: identity` the receiver keeps a delivery when the value of `fields[field]` — for Slack, the sender's member id, which normalize also sets as `actor.id` — is the `id` of some user's identity in this source (`User.IsIn` in internal/store/subscriptions.go read over every user, where `Receiver.hears` reads env files today). `address` and `from` do not apply to this way and are refused with it; the reader likewise refuses `by: identity` with no `hook.keys` field to read, as it refuses a field no key names today. The existing way keeps its spelling (`address: {env: …}`, with `from: operator` to narrow it), so telegram's file and its tests stand unchanged. Matching by identity rather than by env-file address is what lets one workspace hear many people: each mapping is the one line `yardr user identity ada --source slack --id U012AB3CD`, held on the user's record where `yardr thread open --assign` already reads it, and not a second copy of the id in an env file whose other job is telling. Where a mapped delivery is kept, the core adds one field of its own after Normalize, as it derives an envelope's train today (`deriveTrain` in internal/hooks/receiver.go): `fields.speaker` holds the yard user's name, so a subscription may filter on it, the inbox line can name them, and the crew member reading the delivery sees whose it is without a lookup. A delivery kept with no mapping (the `allow` case below) carries `speaker: [outside]`.

A sender no user maps is then one of mckean's three cases, and the declaration cannot choose among them, because the choice is the operator's about their own yard:

- **unknown** — ask the operator (section 3.4). The delivery is dropped as a stranger's is today, and a mapping card is offered. Nothing of it is heard until the operator maps the sender or declines.
- **allow** — heard as an outsider. The delivery is recorded and routed with `speaker: [outside]`; what its words weigh is section 3.5.
- **ignore** — dropped, answered 200, recorded nowhere, counted beside refusals as a stranger's burst is today; no card is offered.

The policy is one word on the hook, set when the hook is made and changed by remaking it, as a hook's `--map` lines are: `yardr hook add slack --source slack --unknown unknown|allow|ignore` (the flag is general, refused for a source whose declaration names no identity hearing, since there is no sender to be unknown). One hook serves the yard, so the policy is per source as the yard runs it — per workspace, Slack being one source per yard in a first version. Per-channel policy is deliberately not in the mechanism: what a channel may do differently is already two standing filters downstream of the gate, a subscription's `--where channel=<id>` choosing which channels reach the yardmaster at all, and the mapping itself, which is per person in every channel at once. Who decides is the operator alone, as for every hook: `hook add` is the operator's command, its run ask is approved by the operator, and no crew member's subscription or mapping changes the gate. The default the first setup takes is open question 1.

### 3.4. The mapping card: "who is this?" in place of "is this you?"

The claim of yardr-68bf asks the operator about their own address and writes it with `user key`. A Slack sender is somebody else's id, so the claim generalises along the axis the bead names — from an address a user is told at, to an identity a user speaks as:

- A dropped delivery in the `unknown` case offers the operator one card per source and sender (where the telegram claim waits one per source and operator): *"A message reached slack from U012AB3CD in channel C012AB3CD (first words: '…'). Who is this?"*, showing the member id, the channel, and at most the first 80 characters of the message, with the message kept nowhere else, exactly as the claim card bounds it, and with the same treatment of outside text as data (`claimPlain` in internal/store/claim.go: control and bidi characters dropped, a value holding any offers no card).
- The card is a new kind of ask beside the run ask, a **mapping ask**, because no one command is being proposed until the operator names the person: its answer is a pick among the yard's users, each labelled with what approving it runs — `yardr user identity <user> --source slack --id U012AB3CD` — plus "someone new" and "nobody — drop messages from this sender". Picking a user writes that identity in the answering write, through the same store function `yardr user identity` calls, under the operator's approval and never from an agent pane, as a run ask runs. That command therefore joins the run-ask list as a new row (it is a crew identity's sibling, `yardr user identity <user> --source <source> --id … [--key …]`, held to the source and the user as the crew row is, and refused with `--rm` as that row refuses it), so the yardmaster may also propose a mapping it already knows — from a name in the channel, say — without waiting for a dropped message.
- "Someone new" chains two standing cards in order: first `yardr user add <name>`, which the operator answers with the new user's name, then the identity card above for that name. The yard does not invent the name: a Slack member id maps to a user the operator names, and the Slack display name normalize cannot read (it is handed no credential, and the event carries no name) is no basis for a yard user name.
- "Nobody" records the member id on a per-source ignore list in the store, so that sender's later messages are dropped in the `ignore` case without a second card, until the operator removes it by the terminal command that lists it (`yardr source show slack` names the ignored ids, as it names what a source still owes). Declining without picking leaves the sender unmapped and offers again on their next message, as an ignored claim holds its slot today.

Approving a mapping writes no env file and tells nobody anything: hearing and telling stay separate, as yardr-5rrs made them for telegram, and a mapped user who also wants the phone road sets `notify` by its own commands where a source can tell (Slack's first version cannot, section 4). The card is still the only identity guard, and with many members the race of yardr-68bf widens honestly: any member who mentions the bot first can be offered as a candidate mapping. The bounds that hold are the ones that held there — the snippet is bounded and sanitised, the approval is the operator's alone, a mapping written meanwhile by hand makes the card stale rather than overwritten, and a wrong mapping is one `yardr user identity <user> --source slack --rm` from undone, with everything the wrongly mapped sender was heard saying still in the record under their member id.

### 3.5. What a heard line weighs

A mapped user's mention is their word as their mail is, within the bounds every chat with the crew already has. The yardmaster, in the session that hears it, discusses it, and where the person asks for work it files and shapes beads for them with `--for <user>` on every create, so the work bills their subscription and stands under their name as originator, exactly as a conversation session files for its person. Three things it never is, each enforced where the yard already enforces it rather than asked of the agent: it answers no ask — asks keep their cards and are answered at the yard, in footplate or `yardr chat`, by the person asked, so a Slack line can neither approve a run, give a secret, nor pick an option, and `mayApprove` and the secret path refuse an agent pane whatever the chat said; it grants nothing — no subscription, identity, grant or setting changes on a Slack say-so, each keeping its own card or terminal command; and it speaks with no credential but the reply's own (section 3.6), so a mapped user directs the yardmaster's conversation and filing, and nothing reaches a source call, a thread state or another yard on their word alone beyond what the yardmaster could already do in its own session. The prime's standing guardrails for telegram carry over word for word: never paste a secret or a credential into the chat, and confirm in the yard what would be confirmed by mail. The residual is telegram's, widened by the number of mapped users: a taken-over Slack account speaks as that user to the yardmaster, with that user's filing rights and no more — strictly less than the operator's stolen device, which can also approve and give secrets, and revocable by removing one identity line.

An unmapped sender in the `allow` case is heard as information, not instructions — the `OutsideMail` mark, applied to a person rather than a yard. Their delivery is routed and may be answered in its thread; the yardmaster may answer questions from what it knows, and may file an outside report only as its own note quoting them (originator the operator's, text marked as an outsider's words, as a bead filed from a peer's mail is that yard's text), never `--for` anyone and never shaped, advanced or landed on their say-so. They are told nothing outside the thread, subscribed to nothing, and mapped by nothing they say. What an `allow` sender can do, in one line: be read and be answered. Everything else waits for a mapping.

### 3.6. Mentions, the bot's identity, and which crew member hears

The bot is a member of the workspace, so mentioning it is mentioning a crew member, by the mechanism that stands. Once the bot token is given, `yardr source check slack` runs the declaration's check call (`auth.test`) and prints the bot's `user_id`; the operator — or the yardmaster proposing it and the operator approving the card — sets:

```sh
yardr crew identity yardmaster --source slack --id U-bot…
```

with no `--key`: Slack mentions arrive as member ids, ids match exactly, and there is no typed handle for a key to forgive, where Linear wants both because its markdown carries a profile key. From then on a text holding `<@U-bot…>` normalises to `mentions: [U-bot…, …]`, the store matches it against the yardmaster's identity, and a subscription of scope `mentions` or `involved` delivers it as `mentioned`. The yardmaster's own posts name it as actor where Slack sends them back, and the standing self-exclusion keeps them out of its inbox.

Who hears is the yardmaster alone in a first version, by two subscriptions the setup leaves standing:

```sh
yardr subscribe yardmaster slack --scope mentions --where type=message
yardr subscribe yardmaster slack --scope all --where type=message --where room=dm
```

`mentions`, not telegram's `all`: a workspace is many channels of other people's talk, and the bot is woken by being addressed, not by everything said where it stands — the subscription is also what keeps channels the yard does not want heard out, with `--where channel=` lines where the operator wants that said per channel of the yardmaster rather than of the gate. A direct message to the bot mentions nobody in its text, so normalize sets `fields.room` to `dm` for a message in a direct-message channel and `channel` otherwise, and the second subscription takes the direct messages whole, as a one-to-one chat is addressed to its other party. Subscribing to Slack's `app_mention` and direct-message events only — not to every channel message — is what keeps one mention from arriving twice, once as each event, and keeps other people's unaddressed channel talk out of the yard entirely; a first version hears mentions and direct messages and nothing else (open question 5). The brakeman subscribes to nothing and is reached through the yardmaster, as in telegram's setup: one recipient means one answer, with no arbitration, and the double-answer hazard of two `mentions` subscriptions on one workspace is the one design yardr-c55e recorded, left to standing instructions if a second crew member is ever subscribed. A Slack thread the yardmaster has answered in is a thread it takes part in, so `involved` would also bring it later messages of that thread that mention nobody; whether follow-ups in a thread should keep reaching it without a fresh mention is open question 4's neighbour and is not subscribed in a first version.

Per person, the hearing lands in the yardmaster's line session, not in a conversation session for that user. That is what the machinery does today: hook deliveries arrive as events, and events go to the line session's slice, while a conversation session's slice takes its person's mail. The mapped user's Slack thread is therefore talked in from the line session, which files for them with `--for` as it files for anyone with no conversation session of their own. Routing a hook delivery to the conversation slice of the user its `speaker` field names is a real second version — it would put each person's Slack talk in the session that already holds their mail and their env — and it is left out (section 4) rather than half-built, because it changes which session a reply's credential and journal belong to.

Replies carry the thread, as section 2.5 promises. normalize sets the envelope's `thread.id` to `<channel>:<parent ts>` (`thread_ts` where the message is a reply, else its own `ts`), `message` to its `ts`, and `parent` to the parent `ts`. `slack/thread reply` calls `chat.postMessage` with that channel and `thread_ts` set to the parent, so `yardr reply <seq> "…"` answers where the mention stood, in its thread; `reply --to <ts>` finds the message among `conversations.replies` and answers under the same parent, by the root-finding `Declared.Reply` already does. `slack/thread read` calls `conversations.replies` for the parent and maps each message to `{id: ts, parent, author: {id: user}, at, body}`, so `yardr thread show <seq>` prints the thread the mention opened, oldest first — the thing telegram cannot do, and the reason Slack threads are worth having. `thread.posted` answers `{}` always: `chat.postMessage` takes no idempotency key and keeps no key of ours to find, so a reply retried after its answer was lost posts twice, the limit telegram already documents rather than a new one. There is no `thread.state`, no `thread.open` and no `link`: a Slack thread has no status, opening one would be posting first to a channel, which is telling by another name, and no bead tracks a channel message.

### 3.7. Setup from cards, and the steps that still need a browser or a terminal

SETUP.md walks it as telegram's does, the operator's name where the trial used one. The yard side is cards the whole way; Slack's side is a browser, because Slack gives a workspace no API by which the yard could create or install its own app, and that boundary is named rather than worked around.

1. **The app, from the pack's manifest, in a browser.** At api.slack.com/apps, Create New App, From a manifest: paste `packs/slack/app-manifest.yaml`, which names the bot user, the scopes (`app_mentions:read`, `channels:history`, `groups:history`, `im:history`, `mpim:history`, `chat:write` — the histories are what `conversations.replies` reads a thread back with), the bot events (`app_mention`, `message.im`, `message.mpim`) and leaves the Request URL to step 6. Install the app to the workspace. Two values Slack shows are copied from there into cards below: the signing secret (Basic Information) and, after install, the bot token `xoxb-…` (OAuth & Permissions). Making the app and installing it are the operator's in Slack's pages; no terminal is involved, and no card can do them, since the yard holds no Slack credential yet by which it could act.
2. **Apply the pack, on a card.** A crew member proposes it and the operator approves: `yardr bead ask <bead> "Add Slack to the yard?" --run "pack apply slack"`. The card shows the declaration whole, as the bht9 card renders every field of it, the two scripts with their checksums among them.
3. **The bot token, on a secret card.** `yardr bead ask <bead> "The Slack bot token" --secret slack --context "OAuth & Permissions in the Slack app, after install: xoxb-…"`. Typed unseen, written by the server to the source's env file; in no note, as every secret ask.
4. **Whose token it is.** `yardr source check slack` (the yardmaster runs it where it may make the read call, else mails the operator the command) prints `user_id`, `team_id` and `bot_id` from `auth.test`: the first is the id step 7 sets, and a token Slack refuses fails here rather than at the first mention.
5. **The public hook address, on a card if it is not already set.** `yardr bead ask <bead> "…" --run "settings set hook_public_url https://<his host>/hooks"`, the setting telegram's setup also needs; the hook's URL is then `https://<his host>/hooks/slack`. The address reaching the yard — a tunnel or funnel in front of `hook_addr` — is networking outside yardr, the operator's as it was in the trial, and is the one piece of plumbing no card carries.
6. **The signing secret and the hook, on two cards.** First `yardr bead ask <bead> "Slack's signing secret" --secret-hook slack --source slack --context "Basic Information in the Slack app."`, then `yardr bead ask <bead> "Set up the Slack webhook?" --run "hook add slack --source slack --unknown unknown"` (the last word is open question 1's to settle). Unlike telegram there is no registration call for the yard to make on approval: Slack takes its Request URL only in its own configuration, so approving the hook stands the endpoint up and nothing is sent anywhere. Then, in the browser, the operator pastes the hook's URL into the app's Event Subscriptions Request URL and saves. Slack sends the `url_verification` challenge of section 3.2; the yard answers it from the hook the card just made, and the app shows verified. Turning Event Subscriptions on, where the manifest did not already, is the same browser visit. This paste is a step Slack leaves no API for; it is in a browser, not a terminal.
7. **The bot's identity, on a card.** `yardr bead ask <bead> "…" --run "crew identity yardmaster --source slack --id <user_id from step 4>"`, so `@bot` is a mention of the yardmaster (section 3.6).
8. **The yardmaster's own credential, for answering.** `yardr reply` speaks with the answering crew member's copy of the bot token. On a card this is yardr-0tew's step (`--secret slack --for-crew yardmaster`), and until that bead lands it is the one yard-side step still done in a terminal, exactly as telegram's step 10 is today: append `SLACK_BOT_TOKEN=…` to the yardmaster's env file at mode 0600 and restart it. The manual way stays written in SETUP.md beside the card, as telegram's step 9 keeps both.
9. **The subscription, and an invitation.** The yardmaster subscribes itself from its pane (section 3.6's line) — an agent step, not an operator terminal — and the operator invites the bot into each channel it should hear (`/invite @<bot>` in Slack), without which Slack sends the channel nothing. A first mention is then the test: it lands in the yardmaster's inbox as

```text
5210 10-17 18:02  hook (mentioned) slack message create by ada (U012AB3CD) on "C012AB3CD:1729156800.000100": @yardr can the shed land on Monday?
```

and `yardr reply 5210 "Noted; I will post here when it lands."` answers in that thread, with `yardr thread show 5210` reading the thread back.

The steps that still need something beyond a card, named whole: creating and installing the Slack app and copying its two secrets out of Slack's pages (a browser); pasting the Request URL into the app and saving (a browser, no API); inviting the bot into channels (Slack itself); the public address reaching `hook_addr` (the operator's networking); and, until yardr-0tew lands, the crew member's credential (a terminal). Everything the yard holds — the pack, both secrets, the hook, the identity, the subscription — is a card or the yardmaster's own subscribe.

## 4. Left out

**Telling a user through Slack (`notify`).** A direct message to a mapped user (`conversations.open` then `chat.postMessage`) would let mail, notifications and asks reach a phone as telegram's telling does. It is a second road for the same telling the yard already does through telegram and pushover, it needs its own address story (a member id is an identity here, not a telling address, and section 3.3 refused to merge the two), and it is not what was asked: the wish is to write to the yard, not to be told by it. The pack therefore names no `notify` key; adding one later touches no hearing machinery.

**Per-channel hearing policy.** The gate of section 3.3 is per hook, and which channels reach the yardmaster is per subscription. A rule that one channel's unmapped senders are allowed while another's are asked about would need the gate to read channel policy per delivery — new store state and a second place the operator keeps the answer — for a difference no trial has asked for. The ignore list is per sender across channels for the same reason.

**Routing to a conversation session per person.** Section 3.6 gives the reason: deliveries would change session, with the reply credential and journal that follow the session, and the line session already files `--for` for a mapped user correctly. It becomes attractive when a yard serves several people heavily by Slack, and it is then one routing rule over the `speaker` field this design already records.

**Socket Mode, and polling.** Slack also offers a websocket the app dials out on, which needs no public address. It would need a held connection in the daemon, an app-level token beside the bot token, reconnect and ordering machinery — a new receiver, against a yard that already takes webhooks from three sources. A yard with no public address is the case that would argue for it, as `getUpdates` polling was telegram's, and it reuses this pack's envelope and scripts when it comes.

**More of Slack than mentions and direct messages.** Reactions, channel joins, edits and deletes, files and images (which arrive as ids the yard cannot fetch without a credential normalize is never handed, as telegram notes pictures rather than fetching them), canvases, huddles, slash commands and any interactive button are all normalised to nothing a subscription is offered in a first version, or noted as bracketed text where a message carries no plain text. Slack's message editing also means an `app_mention` can arrive for an edit; normalize says `create` for a new message and lets an edit stand as its own delivery, deduped by `event_id` as Slack sends it.

**Opening a thread, moving one, and linking a bead to one.** Section 3.6: no status exists to move, opening is telling, and a channel is not an issue. A bead that wants the thread cites its delivery's sequence number, as a telegram chat is cited today.

**A second workspace, a second bot, and Enterprise Grid.** One source is one workspace with one bot token and one signing secret, as one telegram source is one bot. Slack's org-wide ids (`E…` enterprises, members known in several workspaces) are beyond the mapping of section 3.4, whose member ids are workspace ids.

**Answering asks, approving, and giving secrets from Slack.** Left out as telegram left it out, and for Slack's mapped users too (section 3.5): the card remains the place a decision is made, told on whatever road tells that user, answered at the yard.

## 5. Open questions

Each of these is the operator's to decide, and each becomes a separate ask to mckean when the design reaches `decide`, as the decide stage puts them. The proposal above is written so any answer to each can be built; where a step of section 3.7 shows one answer (the hook's `--unknown` word), that is the recommended one below, shown so the walk can be read whole, and it decides nothing.

**1. An unmapped sender: asked about, heard as an outsider, or dropped — and per what?** (a) *unknown (claim)*, as recommended in sections 3.3–3.4: dropped until the operator maps them on the card, per hook (that is, per workspace) with the per-sender ignore list as the only finer grain. It costs the operator a card for each new person who mentions the bot, including passers-by, bounded to one waiting card per sender. (b) *allow*: every mention is heard at once as information (section 3.5's outsider bounds), and mapping only upgrades a known sender's weight. It costs the yardmaster reading strangers' mentions as its ordinary inbox, with the gate reduced to a labelling. (c) *ignore*: only mapped members are ever heard, mapped by the terminal command or a yardmaster-proposed card before they first write. It costs the cold start: nobody new can reach the yard by writing to it. Per-channel variants of any of these cost the per-channel gate of section 4. I would take (a): it is the claim card's own answer, it keeps the inbox to people the operator has named, and (b) stays reachable sender by sender by mapping nobody and answering in the thread — while a yard that wants (b) for a busy support channel can be given it by changing one word on the hook.

**2. A mapped user's word: as their mail, or as information?** (a) *As their mail*, as proposed in section 3.5: conversation and filing with `--for`, never an approval, a secret or a grant. (b) *Information, as an outsider's is*, with filing only as a quoted report: every deed then waits for the same person at the yard, which is safer against a taken-over Slack account and guts the wish for anyone whose yard time is on their phone. The enforced bounds are identical under both (section 3.5); the difference is instruction, and under (b) it is instruction doing all the work. I would take (a): mapping is the operator's act of naming whose word a member's is, and a mapping that changes nothing the yardmaster may do for them is a label, not a mapping.

**3. What may an allowed outsider's words set in motion?** (a) *Be read and answered only*, as proposed: questions answered, nothing filed except a quoted outside report the yardmaster files as its own note. (b) *File as an outsider's report directly*: a mention asking for a bead gets one, originator the operator's, the outsider's text quoted and marked — cheaper for a support channel, and it spends the yard's building queue on unvetted asking, bounded by the yardmaster's shaping, which is where spam becomes a backlog rather than a risk. I would take (a) for a first version and let a channel that proves to be a support queue argue for (b) with its traffic in hand.

**4. Follow-ups in a thread: fresh mention required, or is the thread enough?** (a) *Mention each time* (`--scope mentions`, as set up in section 3.7): the bot is woken when addressed, and a thread it answered in stays quiet until it is addressed again. It costs the mapped user an `@bot` per message, which Slack's own reply flow makes one tap. (b) *The thread is enough* (`--scope involved`): once the yardmaster has answered in a thread it hears the thread's later messages mentioning nobody, as it hears a Linear issue it works on. It costs hearing whole threads in busy channels, including talk between other people in them. I would take (a): a workspace thread is other people's conversation in a way an issue's comments are not, and the mention is the speaker saying "this one is for the yard".

**5. Where may the bot be heard from in a first version: mentions in channels it is invited into and its direct messages, or direct messages alone?** (a) *Both*, as proposed: the workspace case that was asked for, with the channel inviting and the subscription's `--where channel=` as the operator's two existing ways to narrow it. (b) *Direct messages alone*, telegram's shape transplanted: one person talking to the bot at a time, no channel audience for anything the yardmaster says back, and no bystander problem at all — at the cost of the thing Slack has that telegram has not, a room where a team talks with the yard together and a mapped user's colleagues see the answer. I would take (a): the bead's scenario is a workspace mentioning a bot, and a private channel the bot is never invited into is already silence.

## 6. The beads it would become

In the order they would land. Each is built and tested as its depot says (`sh .yardr/test`), with Slack itself replaced by a stand-in or recorded bodies where a step calls it, and every bug found or fixed gets a regression test. No bead names Slack outside `packs/slack` and its tests.

**S1. Core: a signature over timestamp and body, and a challenge answer.** Goal: sections 3.2's two keys for any declared source — `signed` as a template over `{timestamp}` and `{body}`, and `hook.challenge` answered by the receiver after verification. Where to look: `internal/source/hook.go` (`hookVerify`, `hookSignature`, `HookTimestamp`, the reader's refusals), `internal/hooks/declared.go` (`Declared.Verify`, `hookTime`), `internal/hooks/receiver.go` (`serve`, before Normalize), docs/integrations.md "What the service sends in". Done when: a declaration saying `signed: "v0:{timestamp}:{body}"` verifies a signature made over that basestring from the header's raw string and refuses one made over the body alone, a stale timestamp and a bad signature, each by test; `signed: body` and the linear and github declarations verify exactly as before; a verified body matching `challenge` is answered with its challenge value and records no event, an unverified one is refused, and a source with no `challenge` key is unchanged; the reader refuses a template with a missing, repeated or unknown placeholder and one naming `{timestamp}` with no timestamp key. Depends on nothing.

**S2. Pack: slack hears and answers.** Goal: `packs/slack` whole — the declaration of section 3.1, `slack/normalize` and `slack/thread`, the manifest, and SETUP.md as far as the terminal way runs (section 3.7's card steps land with S5). Where to look: `packs/telegram` and `packs/linear` as the patterns, `internal/hooks` golden tests as the harness, `internal/source` for what the reader accepts. Done when: recorded Events API bodies (an `app_mention`, a channel message, a direct message, a threaded reply, an edit, a bot's own message, a message with no plain text) normalise to the envelopes of section 3.6 — thread `<channel>:<parent ts>`, mentions as member ids, `fields` channel, team and sender — each pinned by a golden test; `thread.reply` posts to a stand-in's `chat.postMessage` with the thread's channel and `thread_ts` and answers the posted `ts`, `thread.read` maps a stand-in `conversations.replies` answer oldest first, `thread.posted` answers `{}`; `yardr pack check slack` and an apply in a scratch yard carry the declaration and both scripts; `yardr source check slack` against a stand-in prints the bot's ids. Depends on S1.

**S3. Core: hearing by identity, the unknown policy, and the speaker field.** Goal: section 3.3 — `hear.by: identity`, the hook's `--unknown unknown|allow|ignore`, and `fields.speaker` on a kept delivery. Where to look: `internal/source/notify.go` (`hear`, `ClaimAddress` beside it), `internal/hooks/receiver.go` (`hears` and its env-file reading), `internal/store/hooks.go` and `yardr hook add` for the flag, `internal/store/subscriptions.go` (`User.IsIn`) for the match. Done when: a delivery whose sender is a mapped user's id is kept and carries `speaker: [<user>]`, an unmapped one is dropped with a mapping card offered under `unknown`, kept with `speaker: [outside]` under `allow`, and dropped with none under `ignore`, each by receiver test, and a subscription may filter `--where speaker=<user>` as it filters a hook key; a telegram delivery hears and drops exactly as before, and the reader refuses `by: identity` with an `address` or a `from`, and `--unknown` for a source with no identity hearing. Depends on S1; its pack-level test uses S2's declaration.

**S4. The mapping card, and `user identity` on the run-ask list.** Goal: section 3.4 — the mapping ask, the ignore list, and the run row the card runs. Where to look: `internal/store/claim.go` and `internal/store/runask*.go` (the crew identity row as the sibling), `yardr user identity` in internal/cli, `internal/store/subscriptions.go` (`SetIdentity`), docs/integrations.md and docs/chat.md. Done when: a dropped delivery from an unmapped sender stands one mapping card per source and sender showing the member id, the channel and at most 80 characters of the message; picking a user writes their `slack` identity and the sender's next delivery is heard as theirs; "someone new" chains `user add` then the identity card; "nobody" ignores that sender without a second card and `yardr source show` lists them; a mapping written by hand meanwhile makes the card stale, not overwritten; a typed `--run "user identity …"` proposes the same write on a card and is refused with `--rm`; each by store and receiver tests over fake deliveries, as the claim tests stand. Depends on S3.

**S5. Setup from cards, and the words.** Goal: section 3.7 as the pack's SETUP.md and the docs say it — the manifest's walk, the two secret cards, the hook card with `--unknown`, the identity card, and the yardmaster's subscription — with yardr-0tew's `--for-crew` card where it has landed and the terminal way kept beside it where it has not. Where to look: `packs/slack/SETUP.md`, `packs/telegram/SETUP.md` as the shape, docs/integrations.md (the slack walkthrough beside telegram's), the run and secret ask help. Done when: following SETUP.md in an isolated yard (`contrib/isolated-yard/isolated-yard up`, commands as `D/e …`, a stand-in for `slack.com`) with no operator terminal step beyond the ones section 3.7 names, a stand-in mention reaches the yardmaster's inbox as section 3.7's line and `yardr reply` answers it in its thread; SETUP.md names every browser step and the networking as the operator's, and says the pack hears mapped members and, under the hook's policy, unmapped ones. Depends on S2, S3 and S4, and on yardr-0tew for the credential card alone: without it the bead ships the terminal step written down, and the card replaces it when 0tew lands.

**S6. The yardmaster's Slack hearing, in its role.** Goal: the prime says what a Slack delivery is to the session that hears it — section 3.5's weights, the guardrails carried over from telegram, the line-session hearing of section 3.6, and filing with `--for` — so the behaviour is instructed where the yardmaster reads it, not only designed here. Where to look: packs/starter/roles/yardmaster.md beside its telegram words, docs/crew.md "Speaking in sources" and the slack part of docs/integrations.md. Done when: the role says a mapped member's line is their word for conversation and filing and never answers an ask, that an outsider's is information, and that the bot's credential is never pasted into a chat; a reader following it from a Slack inbox line files, answers and refuses as section 3.5 says. Depends on S5 in use; it may land with S5.

Not made here: telling through Slack, per-channel policy, conversation-session routing, Socket Mode, and a second workspace (section 4), each its own bead when a yard asks for it, on the envelope, identity and speaker field these beads record.
